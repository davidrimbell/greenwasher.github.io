Ultimately, transparency and verification are the only viable strategies to navigate the tightening regulatory environment and maintain consumer confidence.

Greenwashing, or “green washing,” occurs when companies invest more in eco-friendly advertising than in genuinely reducing their environmental impact, creating a disconnect between their narrative and their operations. This practice is driven by the global trend of prioritizing sustainability to enhance reputation and attract consumers willing to pay more for products purportedly eco-friendly. However, this strategy often proves counterproductive, as the lack of genuine alignment can severely damage the brand’s image and generate distrust in the market. The prevalence of this problem is evident in the frequency of misleading, vague, or false claims on websites and products, where most assertions lack accessible evidence or data to support them. This misalignment between marketing discourse and operational reality exposes companies to significant reputational risks, forcing them to face criticism for being unable to substantiate their environmental claims. The omission of truthful information not only deceives consumers but also undermines long-term corporate credibility. To combat this phenomenon, it is essential that organizations adopt transparency, establish measurable commitments, and use third-party certifications based on scientific data. Authentic brands must integrate sustainability throughout their entire value chain year-round, rather than treating it as a one-off campaign. This article is relevant to the study of greenwashing because it illustrates how the communications industry can mitigate deception by promoting honest strategies that respond to the growing demand for genuine corporate responsibility.

Companies ‘greenhushing’ to avoid scrutiny of climate goals, Asic says
Australian regulators warn that "greenhushing," where companies hide climate goals to avoid scrutiny, is effectively a form of greenwashing. This practice allows firms to claim strong ESG credentials without verification, creating a misleading green halo while avoiding the hard work of implementation. By staying silent, corporations evade accountability, making it difficult for stakeholders to assess their true environmental impact. The lack of transparency hinders the sharing of knowledge and prevents meaningful progress toward global climate targets. While some companies publicly support international agreements, their underlying business models often contradict these commitments, particularly in the fossil fuel sector. This discrepancy undermines investor confidence and obscures the reality that existing infrastructure alone threatens to exceed climate limits. Regulators emphasize that robust ESG reporting is essential for transparency and informed investment decisions. They identify vague language, unwarranted labels, and unsupported net-zero targets as primary examples of deceptive behavior. Ultimately, hiding behind silence does not protect companies from criticism; instead, it exacerbates the problem by obscuring the gap between stated policies and actual actions.
