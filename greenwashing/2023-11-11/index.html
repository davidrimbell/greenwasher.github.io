‘Bluewashing’: how ecotourism can be used against indigenous communities - EconoTimes
This article exposes how the concept of ecotourism has degraded from a tool for conservation and community benefit into a superficial marketing label. Large-scale projects now frequently use "responsible" branding to justify mass tourism developments that prioritize profit over ecological integrity and genuine social engagement, often excluding locals from meaningful participation or economic gains. The Maraey resort project in Brazil illustrates this "bluewashing" practice, where multinational corporations mask exploitative actions with sustainability rhetoric. By leveraging prestigious corporate partners and sophisticated PR campaigns, the developers created an illusion of local support while bypassing legal requirements for indigenous consent. This strategy allows firms to proceed with displacing communities under the guise of ethical development, highlighting the dangerous gap between corporate messaging and the reality of land dispossession. This case is critically relevant to understanding greenwashing because it demonstrates how ambiguous regulations and weak enforcement enable corporations to erase indigenous rights in favor of economic interests. It shows that without strict accountability, businesses can exploit the lack of clear social reporting standards to justify the elimination of native populations, proving that virtue signaling often serves to obscure human rights violations rather than prevent them.

Carbon Market Greenwashing Systems Deepen Inequalities in Global South - Experts
The article argues that carbon trading in Africa functions primarily as an exploitative greenwashing mechanism rather than a genuine climate solution. Experts highlight that these markets allow wealthy nations and corporations to continue polluting by purchasing offsets, which effectively serve as pollution permits. This system perpetuates historical inequalities by displacing local communities from their land and transferring the financial burden back to the Global South, rather than delivering meaningful environmental or economic benefits to the affected populations. Critics point out that the voluntary carbon market is riddled with fraud, where false claims about environmental benefits distract from the urgent need for real emission reductions. Rather than reducing global carbon footprints, the system enables high-emission industries to maintain business-as-usual operations. This dynamic enriches middlemen and speculators while offering negligible support to vulnerable communities, turning conservation efforts into a form of neo-colonialism that compromises territorial sovereignty for the sake of corporate liability avoidance. The relevance to greenwashing lies in how carbon credits are marketed as tools for climate justice and development, masking their role in legitimizing continued fossil fuel use. By framing these offsets as eco-friendly investments, the narrative obscures the reality that they do not necessitate actual pollution cuts from major emitters. The article concludes that true climate justice requires addressing loss and damage through direct accountability, rejecting carbon markets as a distraction that perpetuates injustice under the guise of sustainability.

CEO of Swiss carbon offset firm South Pole resigns
Renat Heuberger has resigned as CEO of South Pole following intense criticism over exaggerated environmental claims. This leadership change highlights significant integrity failures within the carbon offset industry, specifically regarding the company's alleged knowledge that a major Zimbabwe project offered far fewer benefits than advertised. Such actions undermine trust in climate solutions and exemplify how corporations may market ineffective offsets while misleading consumers. The controversy has triggered broader internal shifts, including the departure of other senior staff and the announcement of a new chief risk officer. These moves signal an urgent need for rigorous scrutiny of climate projects to restore credibility to the global carbon market. The incident demonstrates how greenwashing involves not just false promises, but the active concealment of evidence that offsets do not deliver promised environmental results. This case is highly relevant to greenwashing because it exposes the gap between marketing rhetoric and actual ecological impact. It illustrates how companies can profit from carbon credits that provide minimal to no real benefit, relying on complex or opaque verification processes to maintain public support. Ultimately, it underscores the critical need for transparency and accountability in corporate climate strategies to prevent deceptive practices that hinder genuine environmental progress.

Mapfre: Greenwashing y falta de ética ambiental
Mapfre has been criticized for its inadequate response to climate change, ranking poorly in international assessments of restrictions on fossil fuels. The company has failed to meet its own pledge to reduce insured emissions and continues to provide significant support for oil and gas projects in Latin America, placing it below the European average. This inaction reflects a lack of environmental ethics and climate ambition, calling into question the credibility of its public commitments. The article’s significance lies in exposing the widespread greenwashing within the insurance sector, where most firms continue to finance the expansion of fossil fuel infrastructure despite this contradicting the scientific goals of the Paris Agreement. Although new coal-fired power plants have become largely uninsurable, the transition remains too slow and superficial. The sector has failed to align with climate science, prioritizing profit over the urgency of the global crisis, thereby perpetuating the very problem it claims to combat. Finally, the mass withdrawal of members from the Net Zero Insurance Alliance illustrates the fragility of voluntary commitments in the face of pressure from fossil fuel interests. Many insurers abandoned the initiative under regulatory threats without achieving their actual targets, demonstrating a disconnect between climate rhetoric and concrete actions. The article concludes that, to be truly sustainable, insurers must align their investments with the 1.5°C target and assume legal responsibility for the costs of climate disasters that communities are now facing.
