News about greenwashing on 2023-03-16


An investigation revealed that the majority of rainforest carbon credits certified by Verra, the dominant global certifier, fail to represent genuine carbon reductions. In response to these gaping loopholes, Verra announced plans to overhaul its certification mechanism by 2025. This scandal highlights the systemic fragility of current offset programs, which have long been criticized by environmental activists for lacking the rigorous checks and balances necessary to verify actual climate benefits. The article argues that the carbon offset industry functions primarily as a revenue stream for big polluters rather than a tool for emission reduction. By allowing corporations to finance vague environmental projects in distant countries, the system creates a loophole for businesses to continue polluting while claiming environmental responsibility. This lack of transparency enables fraudulent claims to pass through, effectively turning carbon trading into a market where the most significant beneficiaries are those contributing least to actual ecological preservation. This issue is critical to understanding greenwashing because it demonstrates how companies exploit consumer goodwill through seemingly charitable contributions, such as flight offset programs. The reliance on unverified paper certifications rather than tangible, on-the-ground results allows firms to present a false image of sustainability. Consequently, the carbon offset market risks becoming a vehicle for money laundering and widespread deception, urging immediate government intervention to replace voluntary, unregulated systems with strict, verified frameworks.

Chevron backing multitude of carbon technologies
Chevron is aggressively positioning itself as a leader in the transition to lower-carbon energy, emphasizing its commitment to achieving net-zero emissions by 2050. By highlighting investments in carbon capture and storage technologies and strategic partnerships across the Asia-Pacific region, the company aims to showcase its technological capability and global collaboration. This narrative serves to reassure stakeholders that it is actively developing scalable solutions beyond its flagship Gorgon project, thereby reinforcing its image as an innovator in sustainable energy practices rather than just a traditional fossil fuel producer. However, the company’s credibility is complicated by its admission of failing to meet earlier capture targets at the Gorgon facility, which necessitated significant financial investments to address the shortfall. This discrepancy between corporate pledges and operational reality introduces a critical layer of scrutiny, as it raises questions about the reliability of the data used to support Chevron’s green credentials. The gap between promised environmental benefits and actual performance suggests that the company may be overstating its progress, a common tactic used to mask continued reliance on high-emission activities while projecting an eco-friendly façade. This situation is highly relevant to greenwashing because it illustrates how energy giants can leverage emerging technologies to create an illusion of immediate sustainability while obscuring past failures and ongoing emissions. By focusing heavily on future partnerships and unproven abatement solutions, Chevron potentially diverts attention from its current environmental impact. This approach risks misleading consumers and investors into believing the company is more environmentally responsible than it truly is, thereby maintaining its social license to operate without implementing the drastic reductions necessary to combat climate change effectively.

Improve transparency, curb greenwashing: how Sebi wants to add more teeth to ESG disclosures
The article highlights the Securities and Exchange Board of India’s initiative to mandate the Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report (BRSR) for the top thousand listed companies, aiming to standardize Environmental, Social, and Governance disclosures. By making these reports compulsory, the regulator seeks to establish a uniform framework that reduces ambiguity in corporate sustainability claims. This structural shift is designed to enhance the reliability of ESG data, ensuring that companies adhere to a consistent set of reporting guidelines rather than relying on voluntary or disparate standards. Central to this regulatory strategy is the emphasis on third-party assurance of sustainability reports to build market credibility. The proposal identifies assurance as the critical mechanism for validating the accuracy of ESG ratings and disclosures. By introducing rigorous verification processes, the framework intends to mitigate the risk of misleading information, thereby forcing companies to substantiate their environmental and social impacts with auditable evidence rather than unverified statements. This development is highly relevant to greenwashing as it directly addresses the core issue of unverified claims. By enforcing mandatory reporting and requiring assurance, the regulatory move aims to eliminate the opacity that allows corporations to exaggerate their sustainability efforts. It forces a transition from superficial marketing narratives to accountable, data-driven transparency, making it significantly harder for entities to engage in deceptive green practices without facing regulatory scrutiny.

Russell Brand is the latest to platform climate conservative Bjørn Lomborg’s ‘reckless’ net-zero cost claims | Temperature Check
The article exposes how charismatic media figures amplify misinformation to obstruct climate action, a core tactic of greenwashing. Russell Brand’s platform, which previously championed social justice, now lends credibility to Bjørn Lomborg, a commentator known for downplaying climate urgency. By hosting such figures under the guise of open debate, these channels legitimize fringe, industry-aligned viewpoints, effectively hijarding public discourse and misleading audiences who trust the hosts’ moral authority. A primary mechanism for this distortion is the deliberate misrepresentation of scientific data and technological capabilities. Lomborg repeatedly cites a discredited, exaggerated cost figure for net-zero policies and falsely claims battery storage is insufficient for renewable grids. This strategy relies on spreading technically flawed arguments that sound plausible to the layperson, thereby creating doubt about the viability of clean energy transitions. Such selective use of evidence obscures the true efficiency and affordability of renewables, serving the interests of those opposed to rapid decarbonization. Furthermore, politicians and pundits propagate myths that renewable energy drives high costs or is inherently unreliable, while promoting expensive, high-emission fossil fuel alternatives. This narrative flips the economic reality, suggesting that investing in hydro storage is wasteful while upgrading coal plants is a sensible economic move. This deliberate confusion allows vested interests to frame necessary climate policies as impractical burdens, masking the continued promotion of dirty energy sources as rational stewardship. This is relevant to greenwashing because it demonstrates how the problem is not just false claims about a product’s environmental friendliness, but the broader manipulation of public opinion to delay systemic change. By spreading disinformation about the cost and reliability of green solutions, bad actors create a false narrative that environmental action is economically ruinous, thereby protecting polluting industries from regulation and maintaining the status quo under the guise of fiscal responsibility.
