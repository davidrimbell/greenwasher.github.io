Unsustainable: The rise of greenwashing in New Zealand - and why we need tougher rules

This article highlights the pervasive issue of greenwashing in New Zealand, where marketing terms like “eco-friendly” or “compostable” have become standard rather than exceptional. Because the country lacks robust regulation and enforcement mechanisms for environmental claims, consumers face significant difficulty distinguishing genuinely sustainable products from those using misleading tactics. This regulatory gap allows companies to capitalize on consumers’ desire to make ethical choices without providing verifiable evidence of their environmental benefits. The consequences of unchecked green claims extend beyond consumer deception to practical environmental challenges, particularly in waste management. Vague labels such as “compostable” often mislead users about proper disposal methods, creating operational nightmares for recycling facilities that handle materials unable to break down under local conditions. Furthermore, research indicates that many so-called compostable items contain toxic additives, undermining their environmental promise and potentially contaminating compost streams. This disconnect between marketing and reality illustrates how greenwashing can actively harm the very ecosystems it claims to protect. The relevance of this topic to greenwashing lies in its demonstration of how unverified claims exploit consumer trust and obscure actual environmental performance. Without stringent oversight or standardized definitions, companies can easily make unsubstantiated assertions that appear credible to the average shopper. The article underscores that true sustainability requires transparency and accountability, whereas current practices allow corporations to prioritize branding over substantive ecological impact, thereby perpetuating a cycle of misinformation and inaction.
