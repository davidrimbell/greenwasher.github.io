Alertan sobre «greenwashing» en productos plásticos
The INTEC warns that marketing plastic products as "ecological" often creates consumer misconceptions by conflating distinct technical categories. Many items labeled as biobased, biodegradable, or compostable fail to meet the specific scientific criteria required for these terms, leading to significant market disinformation and confusion regarding the actual environmental impact of these materials. The core issue lies in the mistaken assumption that biobased plastics are inherently biodegradable or compostable. A material’s renewable origin is independent of its ability to decompose, and biodegradability does not automatically imply compostability. Clarifying these differences is essential to prevent the industry from exploiting ambiguous terminology, ensuring that claims about material origins and degradation rates are scientifically accurate and distinct. This guide is critically relevant to greenwashing because it exposes how vague environmental labels can mislead the public into believing products are eco-friendly when they are not. By promoting standardized definitions and transparent labeling, the research aims to curb deceptive marketing practices, ensuring that claims of sustainability are backed by rigorous evidence rather than misleading impressions.

HDE sound alarm over EU greenwashing directive’s waste impact
The EU Empowering Consumers Directive aims to combat greenwashing by banning products with misleading environmental claims. While intended to protect consumers, industry leaders warn this approach may inadvertently increase waste by forcing the destruction of already manufactured goods. This creates a tension between regulatory enforcement and genuine sustainability goals. Enforcement mechanisms vary, with private bodies in Germany often ignoring recommendations to minimize waste. Consequently, retailers face sudden legal risks and financial losses without clear transition periods. This lack of legal certainty forces companies to choose between compliance and preventing unnecessary disposal of usable materials. Relevance to greenwashing lies in this paradox: strict anti-greenwashing measures might generate physical waste. The article highlights how poorly designed regulations can undermine environmental objectives. True progress requires balancing consumer protection with practical transition options, ensuring that efforts to stop misleading claims do not create new ecological harms through premature product destruction.
