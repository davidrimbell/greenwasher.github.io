¿Qué es el “ecopostureo”? La Comisión Europea alerta sobre el lavado verde
The article examines the European Commission’s new proposal to combat greenwashing, aiming to stop companies from making unfounded environmental claims through mandatory scientific verification and stricter regulations on eco-labels. This initiative responds to significant consumer demand for trustworthy information, seeking to protect buyers from deceptive marketing and ensure fair competition for genuinely sustainable businesses. Critically, the proposal is criticized for failing to prohibit specific misleading terms or establish universal methodologies for verification. Experts argue that without explicit bans on vague claims like "biodegradable," which often merely convert to microplastics, or "implied" visual cues, the rules remain ineffective. They warn that the current framework allows for superficial compliance that does not address the core reality of industrial pollution, leaving consumers vulnerable to continued deception. This case is vital to understanding greenwashing as it highlights the gap between marketing rhetoric and actual environmental impact. It illustrates how companies exploit technicalities, such as claiming reduced emissions per unit while increasing total production, or using self-certified labels to mask intensive farming practices. The controversy underscores the urgent need for rigorous, independent oversight and standardized definitions to prevent businesses from masking their negative ecological footprint with superficial "green" imagery.

Low-carbon or greenwash? Award for Penang South Islands mega project criticised for setting ‘dangerous precedent’
The Penang South Islands project received a “low-carbon” design award from the Malaysian Green Technology Corporation, sparking accusations of greenwashing because the accolade ignored the massive carbon emissions generated during the land reclamation process. The agency only evaluated the planned integration of energy and waste management systems relative to a standard scenario, failing to account for the fossil fuel-intensive nature of constructing artificial islands. This selective assessment highlights a critical flaw in current sustainability labeling, where operational or design efficiencies are celebrated while excluding the profound environmental costs of construction and habitat destruction. Critics argue that this labeling lends false legitimacy to an ecologically damaging project, as the physical act of reclaiming land from the ocean floor entails significant dredging, sand mining, and marine ecosystem disruption. The discrepancy between the award’s focus on future design ideals and the reality of immediate environmental harm illustrates how greenwashing can obscure the true carbon footprint of large infrastructure developments. By ignoring the upfront emissions and social impacts, such awards risk setting a dangerous precedent where superficial “green” features mask substantive ecological damage. This case is relevant to greenwashing because it demonstrates how technicalities in certification criteria can be exploited to market harmful projects as sustainable. The award’s narrow scope allows developers to claim climate action despite ongoing controversies regarding unapproved environmental impact assessments and inadequate compensation for affected local fisheries. Ultimately, the incident underscores the need for comprehensive evaluations that include construction-phase emissions and social consequences, rather than relying solely on designed operational efficiencies to verify a project’s environmental credentials.

Olviden el "greenwashing": ahora el foco está puesto en los errores ecológicos
The article introduces the concept of "green-botching," defined as the poor implementation of well-intentioned environmental measures that, far from helping, end up being counterproductive. Unlike *greenwashing* (exaggerating one’s green credentials) or *green-hushing* (concealing one’s green efforts), this new dynamic arises from clumsy execution that frustrates users and undermines the credibility of the ecological transition, creating a negative narrative for policies that require public support. This phenomenon is critically exemplified in the electric vehicle industry. Although governments have aggressively promoted the sale of zero-emission cars with commercial success, they have failed to synchronize this push with the necessary charging infrastructure. The lack of reliable, accessible, and well-maintained charging stations has replaced range anxiety with widespread frustration, leading to mass complaints and distrust among drivers and manufacturers. This disconnect between the promotion of the technology and the practical reality faced by users is hindering market expansion. The relevance of this text to the study of *greenwashing* lies in how governments and corporations can end up undermining their own environmental agendas through operational negligence. The article warns that these "ecological blunders" not only harm businesses and the image of sustainability but also slow down real progress in combating climate change. It serves as a reminder that good intentions are not enough; without comprehensive and functional execution, green efforts can provoke an adverse public reaction that indirectly benefits polluting industries.

This framework requires lenders to strictly allocate proceeds toward verified activities like carbon reduction and ecosystem protection. It establishes a clear accountability structure where internal checks complement external audits, preventing institutions from falsely claiming ecological contributions without substantive operational changes. These measures are critical to greenwashing discourse because they create enforceable standards for financial claims. By shifting from voluntary promises to regulated verification, the RBI protects investors and markets from deceptive practices, ensuring that "green" labels reflect authentic environmental impact rather than mere marketing narratives.
