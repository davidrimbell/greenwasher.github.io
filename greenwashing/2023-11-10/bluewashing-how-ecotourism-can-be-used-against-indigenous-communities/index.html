‘Bluewashing’: how ecotourism can be used against indigenous communities

The article exposes how sustainability labels like “ecotourism” have morphed into marketing tools, or “bluewashing,” that mask exploitative corporate practices. By branding mass tourism or luxury developments as responsible, multinational corporations obscure the reality of their operations, which often prioritize profit over genuine conservation or social equity. This semantic shift allows firms to present a virtuous image while engaging in activities that contradict their stated ethical values, creating a disconnect between public perception and actual impact. Central to this greenwashing is the violation of indigenous rights and territorial sovereignty. The Maraey project in Brazil exemplifies this, where developers use vague promises of inclusion to justify displacing the Mbyá Guaraní community from their ancestral lands. Despite claiming legal compliance, the project relies on ambiguous agreements and fails to engage meaningfully with the community, illustrating a “logic of elimination.” These practices demonstrate how corporations exploit regulatory loopholes to erase native presence, using environmental arguments to legitimize the removal of local populations who are essential to the land’s ecological and cultural integrity. This case is critically relevant to greenwashing because it highlights the danger of decoupling sustainability rhetoric from human rights and environmental protection. When companies leverage “bluewashing” to legitimize harmful actions, they not only deceive consumers but also undermine the credibility of legitimate sustainability efforts. The article underscores the urgent need for stricter enforcement and authentic stakeholder engagement, revealing how superficial branding serves to minimize risks and shield corporations from accountability while causing irreversible social and ecological damage.
