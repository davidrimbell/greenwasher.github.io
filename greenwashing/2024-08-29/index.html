How Greenwashing Tricks Shoppers and Costs You Money

Stories selected and summarised by AI, not reviewed by a person. Every source is linked.

Many shoppers look for eco-friendly products. But some companies lie about being green. This is called greenwashing. They exaggerate or make up environmental benefits to sell more items. This trick helps these companies, but it hurts buyers who want to spend money wisely.

Companies often get quick money from greenwashing. A study by the University of Cambridge and the University of Oxford found that firms with vague sustainability claims often see their stock price rise. Shoppers become interested, too. These firms save money because they spend on ads instead of real changes. They do not fix their factories or processes. This lets them take customers from competitors who actually try to be sustainable.

However, shoppers face big risks when they fall for this trick. Buyers often pay more for products that are not truly green. Research in the Journal of Consumer Research shows that people pay higher prices for items they think are eco-friendly. Later, they find out the claims were false. This is a waste of money.

Greenwashing also breaks trust. When shoppers find out a brand lied, they stop believing in all sustainable goods. A report by the European Commission showed that 53% of consumers felt misled by green claims. Another 36% suspected greenwashing. This doubt makes it hard for honest brands to succeed. Shoppers must spend more time checking claims. This makes shopping more stressful and tiring.

Real examples show how this works. The Volkswagen emissions scandal is a famous case. The company sold diesel cars as “clean.” But they used software to cheat emissions tests. Buyers who wanted low emissions were cheated. They faced higher costs and felt upset when the truth came out. Also, the Federal Trade Commission found cases where stores called products “eco-friendly” without proof. This led to fines and angry customers.

These stories show that greenwashing gives companies short-term gains. But it hurts consumer trust and honesty. Shoppers in the consideration stage must be careful. Superficial claims often mean a company is not taking real action. By checking for proof instead of just reading labels, buyers can save money. They can also support brands that truly help the environment.
