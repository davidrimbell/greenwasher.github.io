The passage of Paraguay’s Carbon Credits law has sparked controversy due to allegations of irregular procedures and insufficient regulatory frameworks. Critics argue that the legislation’s lack of clarity regarding the principle of additionality creates a high risk of issuing “junk credits” for already protected forests. This regulatory gap implies that entities can profit from conservation efforts that would have occurred regardless, thereby failing to deliver genuine environmental benefits and potentially incentivizing continued deforestation to maintain the value of carbon offsets. Further concerns highlight significant opacity and equity issues within the proposed system. Stakeholders point out severe limitations on transparency, citing restrictive confidentiality clauses and undefined grounds for administrative reserves that undermine public trust. Additionally, economic barriers are highlighted by high certification costs and ambiguous tax structures, which suggest that only large-scale projects may be viable. This dynamic implies a marginalization of local initiatives and small landowners, concentrating benefits among major players and perpetuating social and environmental inequalities under the guise of climate action. These criticisms are directly relevant to greenwashing, as they illustrate how weak legislation can allow companies or governments to claim environmental stewardship while causing actual harm. By enabling the sale of non-additional credits and lacking robust safeguards, the law risks creating a marketplace where financial transactions replace tangible ecological recovery. This disconnect between stated climate goals and implemented mechanisms exemplifies the core of greenwashing: using complex, poorly regulated financial instruments to create an illusion of sustainability without addressing the root causes of environmental degradation.

Opinion: EU Parliament and Council take action against greenwashing
The European Parliament and Council have agreed on new regulations to combat misleading advertising, directly addressing greenwashing by banning vague environmental claims like "eco-friendly" or "natural" without substantial evidence. This mandates that marketers provide rigorous proof for sustainability labels and emissions offset claims, ensuring environmental marketing reflects actual performance rather than deceptive impressions. This move shifts the burden of proof onto companies, forcing transparency in how products impact the climate. Beyond environmental marketing, the rules prohibit practices that encourage premature replacement, such as false claims about repairability or misrepresenting software updates as essential for functionality. By banning durability-limiting features and guaranteeing clear information on product longevity, the legislation targets planned obsolescence. This creates a more honest marketplace where consumers are not manipulated into discarding usable items, fostering a culture of durability over disposability. These regulations are crucial for greenwashing prevention as they establish legal accountability for sustainability assertions. By requiring recognized certification for labels and enhancing guarantee visibility, the EU empowers consumers to distinguish genuine eco-efforts from marketing fluff. This legislative shift not only protects consumer rights but also raises industry standards, making it harder for brands to exploit environmental concerns for profit while misleading the public.

Week in review: Can US afford to fall behind EU on green fashion legislation?
The article highlights a growing legislative divide between the EU and the US regarding sustainable fashion, emphasizing that the EU is aggressively moving toward stricter environmental standards while the US remains lagging. By proposing bans on generic green claims and implementing extended producer responsibility frameworks, the EU is actively curbing misleading environmental marketing. This regulatory push directly targets the core mechanisms of greenwashing by forcing transparency and holding brands accountable for the full lifecycle of their products. In stark contrast, the US has stalled similar initiatives, such as California’s Responsible Textile Recovery Act, due to delays and stakeholder negotiations. This hesitation leaves a regulatory vacuum where manufacturers can potentially continue using vague environmental claims without rigorous oversight or financial responsibility for waste. The absence of comprehensive federal or state-level mandates in the US allows for continued ambiguity in sustainability reporting, which facilitates deceptive marketing practices that would be prohibited under EU guidelines. This disparity is critical to understanding global greenwashing because it creates an uneven playing field for international fashion brands. Companies operating in both jurisdictions may adopt EU-compliant standards only where legally required, potentially using the lack of US regulation to maintain looser marketing practices domestically. Consequently, consumers in the US face higher risks of encountering unverified environmental claims, illustrating how regulatory fragmentation enables and perpetuates greenwashing across borders.
