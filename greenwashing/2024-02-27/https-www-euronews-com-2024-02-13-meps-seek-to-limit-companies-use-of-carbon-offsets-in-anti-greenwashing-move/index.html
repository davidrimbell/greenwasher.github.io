Greenwashing
ES EN

The European Parliament is moving to drastically restrict voluntary carbon offsetting as part of a broader campaign against greenwashing. By mandating that offsets only address residual emissions, the new directive compels companies to prioritize internal reductions, such as energy efficiency and renewable transitions, before relying on external credits. This shift aims to dismantle the misleading narrative that purchasing carbon certificates can substitute for actual operational decarbonization, thereby curbing the practice of using offsets to mask ongoing high emissions. To combat vague or deceptive marketing, the legislation requires explicit environmental claims to be supported by transparent, science-based, and measurable commitments. Companies must now publicly report progress with independently verified interim targets, ensuring that their "net-zero" pledges reflect genuine operational changes rather than hypothetical future plans. This heightened transparency prevents businesses from misleading consumers who might otherwise confuse carbon neutrality with zero emissions, forcing a clearer distinction between a company’s direct footprint and its offsetting strategy. This regulatory evolution is critical because it targets the core mechanism of greenwashing: the disproportionate reliance on unproven or low-integrity carbon credits to achieve climate goals. With major corporations increasingly basing their sustainability reputations on offsetting rather than substantial reductions, these strictures protect consumer trust and environmental integrity. By legally enforcing a hierarchy where direct reduction takes precedence, the EU sets a rigorous global standard that exposes superficial eco-friendly branding and demands authentic corporate accountability.
