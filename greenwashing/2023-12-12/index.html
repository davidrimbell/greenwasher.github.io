Provincial gas utilities in Canada are heavily advertising that "renewable natural gas" will replace fossil fuels, a claim experts identify as deceptive greenwashing. In reality, these renewable sources constitute only a minuscule fraction of the supply, with the vast majority remaining derived from extracted fossil fuels. This widespread misrepresentation serves to delay necessary phase-outs and misleads consumers about the true environmental impact of current energy strategies. While Canada recently amended the Competition Act to require evidence for environmental claims, significant loopholes remain that allow broader corporate sustainability assertions to go unchallenged. The updated regulations likely exclude general industry statements, enabling campaigns that highlight incremental progress while ignoring substantial ongoing emissions. Consequently, major oil and gas coalitions can continue to market net-zero intentions without providing proof that their plans address the bulk of their carbon footprint. The article highlights that Canada’s current legal framework is inadequate compared to more aggressive measures in Europe, where fossil fuel advertising is banned and strict substantiation is mandatory. Experts argue that merely updating the law is insufficient without robust enforcement and proactive investigations into greenwashing. This regulatory gap slows the green transition by allowing fossil fuel companies to maintain their market share through misleading narratives rather than genuine decarbonization efforts.

House panel subpoenas Vanguard, Arjuna in ESG ‘collusion' probe
A congressional committee has issued subpoenas to major investment firms Vanguard and Arjuna Capital, escalating a yearlong investigation into whether their environmental, social, and governance policies violate antitrust laws. The committee argues that these firms may have engaged in collusive agreements to decarbonize assets, potentially restricting investor choice and limiting competition in the oil and gas sectors. This legal pressure highlights a significant political clash over the role of ESG criteria in modern finance, framing sustainability efforts as potentially anti-competitive rather than socially beneficial. The investigation targets coalitions like Climate Action 100+, suggesting that coordinated efforts to reduce emissions among major asset managers could illegally deprive investors of the opportunity to invest heavily in fossil fuels. Lawmakers allege that such ESG-informed decisions limit market output and increase prices, posing risks to American economic freedom. By scrutinizing these alliances, the committee implies that the push for net-zero goals functions as a coordinated restraint on trade, shifting the narrative of sustainable investing from ethical stewardship to potential market manipulation. This development is highly relevant to greenwashing discussions, as it demonstrates how ESG frameworks can be politicized and legally challenged when viewed through an antitrust lens. The conflict underscores the tension between voluntary corporate sustainability pledges and regulatory scrutiny, revealing that "green" investments are not just about environmental impact but also about market power. It suggests that the legitimacy of ESG strategies is increasingly contested, with critics accusing proponents of using environmental goals to exert undue influence over global markets under the guise of moral responsibility.

Preaching sustainability while hawking fast fashion – meet the greenwashing influencers | Sarah Manavis
The article argues that influencers have become a primary vehicle for corporate greenwashing, acting as a protective buffer between fast fashion brands and public backlash. By positioning themselves as sustainability ambassadors for companies like Shein and H&M, influencers lend credibility to vague environmental promises. This strategy allows corporations to deflect criticism while promoting halfhearted eco-initiatives, effectively using the influencer’s perceived authenticity to mask the underlying reality of unsustainable production practices and excessive consumption. Furthermore, many influencers engage in a performative contradiction by cultivating an "eco-adjacent" persona while simultaneously profiting from fast fashion through affiliate links and collaborations. These content creators often promote minimalist or sustainable lifestyles to maintain their audience’s trust, yet their revenue models depend on encouraging continuous shopping. This creates a deceptive narrative where environmental consciousness is used as a marketing tool rather than a genuine commitment, allowing influencers to reap financial benefits from the very industries they claim to critique. The piece concludes that influencer-led sustainable brands, such as Tala, rarely offer true environmental solutions. Instead, they adopt defensive, incrementalist language that frames sustainability as a "journey" rather than an immediate standard, often partnering with non-eco-friendly retailers. This approach normalizes greenwashing by suggesting that imperfect efforts are sufficient. The author highlights the fundamental incompatibility between the influencer economy’s need for constant novelty and the true requirements of sustainability, which demand slowing down and reducing consumption.
