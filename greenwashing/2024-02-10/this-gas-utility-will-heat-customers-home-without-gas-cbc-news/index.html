The article highlights Canada’s struggle to effectively regulate greenwashing, noting that the current enforcement mechanisms are insufficient compared to stricter international standards like those in the European Union. Although proposed amendments to the Competition Act will require companies to substantiate environmental claims with proper testing, critics argue that systemic issues such as limited regulatory capacity allow businesses to evade accountability. This enforcement gap enables companies to withdraw misleading campaigns merely upon complaint, rather than facing immediate penalties, leaving Canada significantly behind global peers in protecting consumers and the environment. A central implication is that the lack of robust oversight undermines public trust in corporate sustainability efforts. With more than half of Canadian consumers skeptical of green claims, the ambiguity surrounding terms like "net-zero" or "carbon neutral" creates a confusing market landscape. This uncertainty not only damages consumer confidence but also discourages legitimate businesses from promoting their genuine eco-friendly initiatives for fear of being falsely accused of deception. Consequently, the status quo perpetuates a cycle of doubt where authentic efforts are overshadowed by questionable marketing practices. This situation is critical to understanding greenwashing because it reveals how regulatory weaknesses facilitate deceptive advertising on a systemic scale. Without a specialized watchdog unit and stronger, proactive enforcement, the Competition Bureau operates in a reactive "Whac-A-Mole" fashion, addressing isolated complaints rather than preventing widespread misinformation. The article underscores that meaningful progress requires not just updated laws, but the institutional strength to enforce them consistently, ensuring that environmental claims are verified and that the transition to a sustainable economy is built on transparency rather than deception.
