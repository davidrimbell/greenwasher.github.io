Column: Green hydrogen or greenwashing? Mojave water scheme takes new twist

Cadiz Inc., long criticized for plans to drain a fragile Mojave groundwater aquifer, is attempting to rebrand its controversial project as a climate-friendly enterprise by pivoting toward green hydrogen production. By partnering with a Spanish developer to use solar energy for splitting water molecules, the company aims to position itself as a purveyor of clean fuel, leveraging political connections and recent agreements involving former Keystone XL pipeline materials to legitimize its operations. This strategic shift highlights classic greenwashing, where a company with a history of environmental opposition adopts eco-friendly labels to distract from its core, potentially destructive activities. Critics argue that the green hydrogen narrative serves as a "metaphorical Febreze," masking the fundamental issue of extracting ancient water from the desert to profit wealthy coastal cities and private investors. The emphasis on renewable energy sources obscures the ecological damage caused by depleting local aquifers that sustain wildlife and tribal communities. The relevance to greenwashing lies in Cadiz’s use of environmental justice and clean energy rhetoric to bypass regulatory hurdles and secure political favor. By claiming majority Indigenous ownership and pledging benefits to low-income communities, the company creates an appearance of social responsibility that contradicts the widespread concerns of conservationists. This case demonstrates how corporations can exploit the demand for sustainable solutions to legitimize projects that remain ecologically hazardous and economically driven.
