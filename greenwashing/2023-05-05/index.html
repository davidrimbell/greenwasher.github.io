‘A sea of misinformation’: FTC to address industry greenwashing complaints
The US Federal Trade Commission is updating its Green Guides to combat widespread greenwashing by major corporations, aiming to clarify when deceptive environmental marketing violates federal law. This regulatory shift responds to years of complaints regarding misleading claims from industries such as fossil fuels, agriculture, and plastics, where vague terminology has allowed companies to exploit growing consumer demand for sustainability. By establishing stricter definitions, the FTC seeks to empower itself with stronger legal standing to prosecute businesses that engage in organized disinformation campaigns. A central focus of this update is dismantling the "elaborate myths" surrounding plastic recyclability and carbon offsets, which critics argue are sophisticated marketing schemes designed to mislead the public. Industry leaders have long promoted narratives like universal plastic recyclability or the efficacy of natural gas as a climate solution, despite evidence showing these practices are often financially unviable or environmentally unsubstantiated. The new rules aim to force transparency by requiring verifiable data for net-zero claims and restricting the use of generic terms like "clean" or "natural" for polluting products, thereby exposing the gap between corporate advertising and actual environmental impact. This initiative is crucial to the fight against greenwashing because it addresses the power imbalance created by complex, often unverified claims that leave consumers badly misled about their ecological footprint. With previous guidelines dating back decades, the proliferation of fictitious marketing has thrived in a regulatory vacuum, allowing polluters to rebuild reputations without accountability. Strengthening these guidelines not only protects consumers from manipulation but also establishes a federal baseline for truthfulness, countering the insidious practice of capitalizing on environmental concerns through vague and unprovable assertions.

FTC Considers Updates for Green Guides to Address Greenwashing - EcoWatch
The FTC is updating its Green Guides to combat greenwashing, addressing the dangerous gap between corporate marketing claims and actual environmental impact. This review aims to clarify misleading labels on recyclables and renewable products, ensuring that consumer understanding aligns with reality. By strengthening these non-binding guidelines, the agency seeks to provide greater legal backing for cases against companies exploiting vague sustainability terminology. A primary focus is the widespread deception surrounding recyclability, particularly given the low rate of actual plastic recycling in the U.S. The guides will also scrutinize vague terms like "net zero" and carbon offsets, which often mask a company’s true carbon footprint. Environmental groups argue that these claims frequently mislead consumers striving to reduce their personal environmental impact, allowing corporations to appear climate-friendly without making substantial operational changes. This update is highly relevant to greenwashing because it directly targets the misinformation that allows businesses to profit from false eco-friendly images. By refining standards for carbon offsets and organic claims, the FTC aims to restore consumer trust and prevent companies from leveraging ambiguity to sell unsustainable products. These changes represent a critical step toward holding brands accountable for genuine environmental responsibility rather than superficial marketing tactics.

The coming crackdown on corporate greenwashing - Taipei Times
The article argues that widespread corporate greenwashing is facing significant legal and regulatory backlash, transforming vague sustainability marketing into a high-risk liability. New rules in jurisdictions like the UK and France now strictly define carbon neutrality, mandating that claims reflect absolute reductions across a product’s entire lifecycle rather than relying on mere offsets. This shift dismantles the ability of companies to use superficial campaigns to obscure continued pollution, as regulators increasingly demand transparency and penalize misleading advertising. Concurrently, a surge in lawsuits and critical reports exposes the gap between corporate net-zero pledges and actual practices. Major industries, including oil and cement, are being sued for deceptive claims, while audits reveal that most existing plans fail to align with scientific requirements for immediate, substantial emission cuts. These developments highlight that superficial commitments, often bolstered by questionable offset schemes, are no longer sufficient to satisfy legal standards or consumer expectations, forcing firms to substantiate their environmental narratives with credible, verified data. This trend is relevant to greenwashing because it establishes a concrete framework for accountability, moving the issue from voluntary corporate social responsibility to enforced legal compliance. The UN’s recommendations and international standards provide the necessary criteria to distinguish genuine climate action from deception, ensuring that net-zero targets are compatible with global climate goals. Ultimately, the article suggests that without rigorous verification and binding fossil fuel phase-outs, corporate climate leadership remains an illusion, making regulatory scrutiny the primary tool for exposing and preventing greenwashing.
