‘Biggest, baddest’ – but is it the cleanest? World’s largest cruise ship sets sail

The launch of the world’s largest cruise ship has intensified scrutiny on the cruise industry’s environmental claims, revealing a stark contrast between marketing and ecological reality. Although the vessel is promoted as a sustainable innovation powered by liquefied natural gas, experts argue this represents a significant greenwashing effort. The industry is relying on transitional fuels that fail to address the true scope of carbon emissions, misleading the public into believing they are adopting cleaner technologies when they are merely extending reliance on fossil fuels. Central to this critique is the issue of methane slip, where unburned methane leaks from engines, resulting in greenhouse gas emissions significantly higher than those of conventional marine fuels. Because methane is far more potent than carbon dioxide, labeling liquefied natural gas as a clean solution exacerbates climate change rather than mitigating it. This strategy allows cruise lines to delay the adoption of genuinely low-emission alternatives like hydrogen or methanol, prioritizing short-term operational flexibility over long-term environmental accountability. Furthermore, the expansion of megaships necessitates destructive port infrastructure that damages marine ecosystems, contradicting broader sustainability goals. As major ports restrict cruise access due to health and environmental concerns, the industry’s push for larger vessels appears fundamentally misaligned with ecological preservation. This case highlights how the sector uses selective terminology to mask its substantial carbon footprint, underscoring the urgent need for transparent, science-based regulations rather than voluntary industry pledges.
