'Nature positive' isn't just planting a few trees – it's actually stopping the damage we do

The article argues that while the "nature positive" movement aims to halt and reverse environmental degradation, its sudden popularity carries a significant risk of greenwashing. Without strict guardrails, corporations and governments may exploit the term to generate positive public relations while continuing business practices that harm ecosystems. The primary danger is that vague commitments to increasing overall "nature" distract from the urgent need to stop specific, severe losses of critical habitats and species, effectively masking continued environmental failure. To prevent misuse, the author emphasizes that true nature positive outcomes require rigorous adherence to mitigation hierarchies, where avoidance of damage is prioritized over compensation. It is crucial to recognize that many ecological losses, such as the destruction of old-growth forests, are irreversible and cannot be genuinely offset by creating different types of biodiversity elsewhere. Furthermore, organizations must account for their entire supply chain footprint rather than just direct impacts, ensuring that restoration efforts are substantial and not merely symbolic substitutes for ongoing destruction. Ultimately, the article asserts that nature positive initiatives are only effective if they translate into tangible action rather than serving as marketing tools. Achieving genuine ecological recovery demands a societal shift where economic priorities no longer supersede environmental protection. By focusing on these fundamental principles, stakeholders can ensure the movement drives real restoration and prevents the dilution of conservation efforts into empty rhetoric, thereby safeguarding the biosphere for future generations.
