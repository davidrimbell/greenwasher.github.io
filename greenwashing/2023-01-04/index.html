India’s national strategy for achieving net-zero emissions by 2070 appears to overlook the substantial potential of blue carbon, despite its critical role in climate mitigation. While the country’s submitted long-term low-carbon development strategy fails to emphasize these resources, coastal ecosystems such as mangroves, tidal marshes, and seagrass meadows offer a highly efficient method for sequestering atmospheric carbon. These marine habitats store carbon at rates significantly higher than terrestrial forests per unit area, representing an underutilized asset in India’s broader environmental policy framework. The environmental and economic benefits of preserving these ecosystems extend far beyond carbon storage. This oversight is relevant to greenwashing because it highlights a discrepancy between the proclaimed ambition of climate neutrality and the actual inclusion of proven, high-impact mitigation strategies. Engineering the future: How to spot greenwashing
The article argues that greenwashing often manifests through vague marketing of minor sustainability efforts that lack systemic impact. Companies frequently highlight specific products as groundbreaking solutions while ignoring their broader operational footprint or the unavailability of these technologies. This selective promotion misleads consumers by presenting isolated, often limited initiatives as comprehensive strategies for environmental change, thereby distracting from the need for fundamental business transformations. Furthermore, greenwashing exploits comparative marketing and historical context to exaggerate environmental benefits. Firms may rebrand long-standing features as new innovations or compare their products against inferior alternatives to appear superior, while omitting critical data regarding wider ecological impacts. Without transparent, standardized metrics like Environmental Product Declarations, these claims obscure the reality that some "green" products may require significantly more resources or generate higher overall emissions than previously assumed. Ultimately, genuine sustainability requires a holistic assessment of trade-offs between carbon benefits and other environmental costs, such as embodied carbon and resource usage. The article emphasizes that without rigorous, numbers-based verification, marketing hype likely conceals insignificant or even harmful practices. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for recognizing greenwashing, as it reveals how superficial claims often mask the complex realities of industrial production and consumption.

The fuel of the future
The article argues that while the current hype surrounding green hydrogen risks becoming a tool for greenwashing—masking inefficient fossil-fuel-based alternatives or enabling ecological exploitation—it remains an indispensable component of the global transition to renewable energy. This ambiguity highlights the danger of viewing hydrogen as a mere techno-fix for high-consumption lifestyles rather than addressing the root causes of excessive resource use, potentially allowing industries to continue unsustainable practices under the guise of sustainability. However, green hydrogen is critical for decarbonizing sectors where direct electrification is not yet viable, such as heavy shipping, aviation, and steel production. The text emphasizes that without clean hydrogen, achieving net-zero emissions by mid-century is practically impossible. Yet, efficiency concerns must be weighed against its necessity, as electric batteries and heat pumps remain superior for many other applications. The focus should therefore remain on deploying hydrogen strategically in hard-to-abate industries rather than broadly replacing existing infrastructure inefficiently. To prevent green hydrogen from becoming a form of "green extractivism," the article stresses the need for strict ethical standards, territorial planning, and community consent in producing nations. It calls for equitable trade relationships where importing countries, like Germany, support sustainable development and local value creation in the Global South. By prioritizing social and environmental safeguards over unchecked expansion, international trade in hydrogen can foster inclusive prosperity rather than exacerbating global inequalities.
