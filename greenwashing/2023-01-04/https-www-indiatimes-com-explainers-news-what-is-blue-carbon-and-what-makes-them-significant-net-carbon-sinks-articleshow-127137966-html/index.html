Greenwashing
ES EN

India’s national strategy for achieving net-zero emissions by 2070 appears to overlook the substantial potential of blue carbon, despite its critical role in climate mitigation. While the country’s submitted long-term low-carbon development strategy fails to emphasize these resources, coastal ecosystems such as mangroves, tidal marshes, and seagrass meadows offer a highly efficient method for sequestering atmospheric carbon. These marine habitats store carbon at rates significantly higher than terrestrial forests per unit area, representing an underutilized asset in India’s broader environmental policy framework. The environmental and economic benefits of preserving these ecosystems extend far beyond carbon storage. Blue carbon habitats naturally filter pollutants, improve water quality, and prevent eutrophication, thereby supporting healthy fisheries and local economies. Globally, initiatives are already working to establish policies and financial mechanisms that protect these vital zones, yet India’s current planning documents do not adequately reflect the urgency or opportunity to integrate these nature-based solutions into its national climate commitments. This oversight is relevant to greenwashing because it highlights a discrepancy between the proclaimed ambition of climate neutrality and the actual inclusion of proven, high-impact mitigation strategies. By neglecting blue carbon in official documentation, India risks presenting an incomplete picture of its environmental progress. True sustainability requires leveraging all available scientific opportunities, and the absence of this key component suggests a potential superficiality in the stated dedication to comprehensive, low-carbon development, undermining the credibility of the nation’s climate claims.
