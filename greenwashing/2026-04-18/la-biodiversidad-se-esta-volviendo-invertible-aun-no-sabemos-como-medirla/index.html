La biodiversidad se está volviendo invertible; aún no sabemos cómo medirla

The article identifies a critical structural flaw in the current approach to financing biodiversity: the reliance on activity-based metrics, such as the number of trees planted, rather than verifiable ecological outcomes. This disconnect prevents the creation of a functional market, as financial actors cannot distinguish between projects that generate real impact and those that merely craft compelling narratives. Consequently, this ambiguity creates fertile ground for greenwashing, allowing companies to claim environmental responsibility without delivering measurable restoration, thereby distorting capital allocation and eroding investor trust. The persistence of this issue stems from a fragmented landscape of standards, where ecological rigor and financial utility rarely intersect. Many existing frameworks either oversimplify biodiversity for financial use or fail to translate complex ecological data into investable signals. This lack of an integrated, common language specifically hinders regions like Latin America, which holds immense natural wealth but lacks the measurement infrastructure needed to attract capital. The result is a paradox in which trillions continue to fund degradation simply because robust signals for protection do not yet exist. Greenwashing flourishes in this vacuum of weak metrics, making the adoption of verified, outcome-based reporting essential for market integrity. To prevent the emergence of a speculative market driven by reputation risks rather than results, stakeholders must prioritize interoperable data systems and standardized verification. Only by shifting focus from implementation activities to actual ecological change can the sector build the trust necessary to scale investment. This transition is crucial to ensuring that biodiversity finance delivers tangible environmental benefits rather than serving as a vehicle for superficial branding.
