High ESG rankings can mask underlying corporate governance failures when assessment frameworks prioritize positive initiatives in specific areas, such as environmental sustainability, while overlooking non-compliance in other critical sectors. This imbalance occurs when industry-specific weightings dilute the significance of governance risks, allowing companies to achieve strong scores despite significant internal control deficiencies. Consequently, a composite score alone provides an incomplete picture of true corporate accountability. To combat this, experts argue that investors must look beyond simple ratings and examine the depth of risk management, including policy implementation, target setting, and year-on-year progress. The rise of mandatory independent audits and rigorous regulatory oversight, such as those introduced by SEBI, aims to enforce transparency and verify the actual impact of corporate actions. These measures are essential for distinguishing genuine sustainability efforts from superficial claims, thereby reducing ambiguity in the market. This regulatory push is directly relevant to greenwashing because it addresses the core issue of unsubstantiated environmental claims by requiring third-party assurance of ESG data. By mandating verification and linking financial incentives to real carbon reduction targets, regulators help ensure that companies are held accountable for their promises. This shift moves the focus from marketing-driven labels to verified performance, protecting investors from misleading disclosures and fostering a more honest ESG landscape.

Coal giant scrambles to run on gas and hydrogen in South Africa
Sasol, South Africa’s second-largest emitter, faces significant skepticism regarding its ability to achieve net-zero emissions by 2050. While the company pledges a green transition, its current strategy relies heavily on swapping coal for natural gas, a move critics deem unrealistic and contradictory to long-term climate goals. This reliance on fossil fuels, despite the ambition to become a major renewable energy buyer, highlights a disconnect between public sustainability targets and operational realities, raising concerns about the authenticity of its environmental commitments. The transition is further complicated by economic vulnerabilities and logistical hurdles. Sasol remains deeply tied to coal, with its primary facility generating emissions comparable to major global oil corporations. Efforts to secure sufficient natural gas supply through exploration in Mozambique or future imports are fraught with financial risks, particularly if oil prices fluctuate. Additionally, South Africa’s unstable power grid and lack of renewable infrastructure hinder the deployment of necessary clean energy solutions, exposing gaps between planned investments and practical implementation capabilities. Ultimately, this case illustrates a classic example of greenwashing, where ambitious net-zero promises are undermined by vague accountability and continued dependence on high-carbon activities. Activists argue that the lack of measurable, credible plans allows Sasol to maintain profitability from dirty fuels while appearing environmentally responsible. This discrepancy between stated goals and tangible action serves as a cautionary tale for corporations claiming to lead the energy transition without undergoing substantive structural changes.

Por fin, no más greenwashing en la moda
The article concludes that the sudden disappearance of so-called “green fashion” from store shelves signals the end of widespread greenwashing in the industry, driven by stricter regulations rather than corporate benevolence. The author argues that major brands previously exploited consumer goodwill by labeling fast fashion items as eco-friendly, creating an inherent contradiction between rapid production cycles and environmental sustainability. This deceptive marketing allowed companies to profit significantly while misleading the public about the true ecological impact of their garments. Underneath the pastoral imagery and vague eco-labels lay a reality of exploitative labor practices and heavy environmental damage. The text highlights that these products were primarily made from petroleum-based materials and toxic dyes, contradicting any claim of sustainability. Despite existing European guidelines since 1992, companies ignored strict standards because compliance was less profitable than deception. The narrative emphasizes that this ingenuity in lying was purely financial, disregarding the well-being of workers and the planet in favor of maximizing short-term gains. Relevance to greenwashing lies in the clear demonstration of how vague claims and aesthetic marketing were used to disguise unsustainable and harmful production methods. The article warns that while current regulations have forced transparency, the underlying lack of ethics remains. Consumers are urged to remember these deceptions and boycott brands that previously engaged in such practices, recognizing that without sustained pressure and ethical vigilance, corporations will likely resume deceptive tactics whenever regulations loosen. This serves as a cautionary tale about the persistent nature of greenwashing and the necessity of critical consumer awareness.

WATCH — Think a company is helping the environment? It might be greenwashing
Companies frequently use terms like “eco-friendly” to mislead consumers, a practice known as greenwashing. This deception aims to capitalize on environmental concerns to drive sales, obscuring the true nature of their products or practices. Understanding this manipulation is crucial for identifying deceptive marketing. By recognizing these tactics, consumers can make more informed decisions rather than falling for superficial claims that prioritize profit over genuine sustainability. This topic highlights the importance of critical scrutiny in modern advertising. It serves as a vital reminder that labels do not always reflect reality, empowering individuals to demand transparency and authentic environmental responsibility from corporations.
