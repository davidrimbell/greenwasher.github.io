‘Reactive’ hiring for ESG roles plagues a nascent green fintech industry in Asia: experts
The rapid expansion of sustainable finance in Asia is hindered by a severe talent shortage, forcing companies to repurpose generalist finance professionals into ESG roles. This "competence greenwash" occurs when underqualified staff, lacking specialized knowledge in both finance and sustainability, are promoted to meet market demand. This practice risks superficial integration of ESG principles rather than genuine expertise, potentially leading to ineffective strategies and compliance failures as regulations tighten. The industry is currently characterized by a lack of strict standards, allowing almost any product to be marketed as sustainable with minimal oversight. However, as central banks in regions like the EU and Singapore begin cracking down on regulatory loopholes, the consequences for non-compliance will become more severe. This shift exposes the danger of hiring inexperienced personnel for compliance roles, creating a critical challenge as the market moves from a forgiving environment to one demanding rigorous adherence to defined green standards. Consequently, there is an urgent need for professionals who possess a unique intersection of financial acumen, technological skills, and deep ESG domain knowledge. The current disconnect between these skill sets means the industry lacks a cohesive playbook to manage large data gaps and make informed investment decisions. Without addressing this expertise deficit, the sector risks undermining its own credibility and failing to deliver meaningful environmental progress despite growing consumer demand for responsible investing.

‘Shein is targeting colleges’: the student battling fast fashion’s ‘hypocritical’ scholarships
This article highlights the controversy surrounding FIDM’s partnership with Shein, illustrating how corporate-sponsored scholarships can serve as a mechanism for greenwashing. By associating with a brand notorious for labor abuses, copyright infringement, and massive environmental waste, educational institutions risk legitimizing unethical practices. This strategy allows companies like Shein to rehabilitate their public image and build trust among younger demographics, effectively distracting from their harmful business operations through calculated public relations efforts rather than genuine sustainability initiatives. The core implication is that these collaborations function primarily as marketing investments for corporations rather than altruistic aid. While students may receive financial support, the primary beneficiary is the sponsoring company, which gains significant credibility and access to new markets. This dynamic reveals a critical aspect of greenwashing: the co-opting of educational and social institutions to create an illusion of corporate responsibility, thereby manipulating consumer perception and obscuring the reality of the brand’s negative environmental and social impact. The article is relevant to greenwashing because it exposes the symbiotic relationship between controversial corporations and prestigious institutions. It demonstrates how "sustainability" claims are often hollow, used to offset widespread criticism and maintain market dominance. The student-led backlash underscores the growing awareness that financial partnerships do not equate to ethical alignment, urging a reevaluation of which brands receive institutional endorsement and challenging the narrative that such sponsorships reflect genuine industry progress.

Companies are claiming to be 'plastic neutral.' Is it greenwashing?
Major corporations are increasingly purchasing plastic credits to claim “plastic neutrality,” asserting that funding waste collection in developing regions offsets their own plastic production. This system allows companies to balance their environmental impact on paper by paying for the removal of equivalent plastic waste elsewhere, a strategy promoted by various unregulated initiatives and adopted by major brands seeking to demonstrate corporate responsibility. However, environmental experts argue this approach is a sophisticated form of greenwashing that prioritizes waste management over genuine reduction. By equating the cleanup of existing pollution with the continued production of new plastic, these credits enable polluters to burnish their reputations without addressing the root causes of plastic waste. Critics warn that this mechanism allows industries to justify ongoing fossil fuel extraction and manufacturing, effectively masking the continuous environmental harm caused by producing single-use plastics. The core controversy lies in the fact that plastic credits may facilitate legal and marketing loopholes rather than solve the crisis. As the industry shifts toward vague “contribution” labels to avoid litigation, the fundamental issue remains that cleanup efforts are futile without strict caps on production. Ultimately, these schemes distract from the urgent need to halt plastic manufacturing, serving the petrochemical industry’s interest in maintaining high output levels while projecting an image of sustainability.
