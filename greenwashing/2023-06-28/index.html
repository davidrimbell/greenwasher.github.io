ASIC Chair Joe Longo emphasizes that greenwashing remains a critical enforcement priority, with regulators actively policing misleading sustainability claims under existing securities laws rather than waiting for specific legislation. Companies are warned against four key problematic behaviors: unfounded net zero targets, the misuse of vague terms like “clean” or “carbon neutral,” inconsistent application of investment screens, and inaccurate fund labeling. The regulator insists that valid sustainability claims require robust governance, board confidence, and clear integration of environmental data into financial reporting structures to avoid deceptive practices. A significant emerging concern is “greenhushing,” where companies reduce or cease ESG disclosures due to uncertainty over reporting frameworks or fear of regulatory scrutiny. Despite acknowledging the lack of fully enacted mandatory disclosure rules, ASIC signals it will not hesitate to enforce current obligations against both overt greenwashing and the strategic withholding of information. This stance highlights a dual regulatory focus, ensuring that the absence of detailed statutory requirements does not create a loophole for companies to obscure their true environmental impact or fail to substantiate their sustainability efforts. This aggressive regulatory posture is relevant to the broader global context of greenwashing enforcement, mirroring similar actions by European and UK authorities who are tightening scrutiny on marketing communications and investment labels. The trend demonstrates that even in the absence of comprehensive laws, existing consumer and securities protections are being leveraged to penalize misleading claims. For investors and companies alike, this underscores that vague or unsubstantiated sustainability narratives are increasingly untenable, requiring transparency and evidence-based disclosures to maintain legitimacy and avoid severe legal repercussions.

European ESG rating regulation aims to restore credibility in the face of greenwashing, but the problem runs deeper than the mere mis-selling of funds. The text warns against deceptive practices such as “impact washing,” where companies refinance existing assets without generating real climate benefits, and “competence washing,” due to inadequate training in sustainable finance. This underscores the need to go beyond regulatory transparency to address the lack of rigor in defining science-based targets. Confidence in climate-responsible investment is undermined by the concentration of emissions in a few key industries and the difficulty of measuring the real impact of other sectors. Although there is debate over the imposition of common standards and risk disclosure, the complexity of creating a static global framework is widely acknowledged. The relevance of data varies by sector and over time, creating tensions between investors’ pressure for comprehensive information and companies’ actual ability to produce reliable data without incurring prohibitive costs. To effectively combat greenwashing, it is proposed to move away from the perfectionism of detailed accounting toward more realistic approaches, such as top-down modeling of supply chains. Although less precise, these techniques provide a comprehensive view of climate risks through stress testing with internationally agreed scenarios. The true solution does not lie solely in cumbersome disclosure, but in balancing transparency with practicality, ensuring that assessments accurately reflect companies’ environmental and social impact.

New carbon accounting rules for cos target 'greenwashing' - ET EnergyWorld
New global reporting standards aim to eliminate corporate greenwashing by establishing reliable, uniform metrics for greenhouse gas emissions. Previously, inconsistent data and a lack of common guidelines allowed firms to exaggerate their environmental credentials, misleading stakeholders. The International Sustainability Standards Board introduces these rules to create a shared language for disclosing climate risks. By ensuring transparent and accurate sustainability information, the standards seek to rebuild trust in financial markets and support more informed investment decisions. This initiative is crucial for combating greenwashing because it removes the ambiguity companies previously exploited. Mandatory or voluntary adoption of these rigorous norms ensures that climate claims are substantiated by comparable, high-quality data rather than vague marketing.
