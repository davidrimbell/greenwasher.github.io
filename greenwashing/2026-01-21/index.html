How to Become a Tree - Longreads
The emerging green death care industry faces scrutiny regarding the validity of its environmental claims. While methods like human composting and alkaline hydrolysis promise a gentler return to the earth, these assertions often lack comprehensive examination, raising significant concerns about potential greenwashing within this nascent market. Evaluating any single disposal method as definitively "green" is highly complex due to conflicting environmental metrics. A technique may minimize carbon emissions while significantly increasing water consumption, or perform well in one region but poorly in another. Furthermore, hidden impacts within supply chains, such as the resources required to grow organic matter for composting, complicate the assessment of true ecological benefit. This article is relevant to greenwashing because it highlights how marketing simplifies nuanced environmental trade-offs. By emphasizing isolated benefits like reduced carbon footprints, companies may obscure other negative impacts, such as transport emissions or resource intensity. This selective reporting creates a misleading narrative of sustainability, demonstrating how the industry might exploit environmental concerns to sell services without delivering holistic ecological integrity.

Tobacco companies targeting gen Z through social media
The article highlights a concerning resurgence in tobacco use among younger generations, driven by major tobacco companies pivoting their strategies to target Gen Z via social media. Despite overall smoking rates being at historic lows, experts warn that platforms are glamorizing nicotine products through influencers and popular culture, creating a false perception that vaping and smoking are widespread and socially acceptable behaviors. This digital re-normalization threatens to undo decades of health progress by exploiting youth curiosity and misunderstandings about addiction risks. A critical component of this threat is the industry’s use of greenwashing, where tobacco corporations leverage sustainability awards or false environmental claims to present themselves as ethical corporate citizens. By marketing products as "plant-based" or "greener" alternatives, these companies create dangerous misconceptions that certain nicotine products are less harmful to the environment or health. This deceptive marketing exploits the public’s growing eco-consciousness, allowing firms to distract from the severe ecological and health damages caused by their core products while gaining unwanted legitimacy. This narrative is directly relevant to greenwashing because it illustrates how industries repackaging harmful products use environmental rhetoric to obscure their negative impact. The article demonstrates that greenwashing is not merely about eco-labels but is a strategic tool to re-normalize addiction and manipulate public perception. It underscores the urgent need to scrutinize corporate sustainability claims when applied to inherently damaging industries, revealing how such tactics can mislead consumers and undermine public health initiatives.
