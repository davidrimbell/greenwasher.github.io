Green Claims Directive: Why Silence Hurts Sustainable Shopping

Shoppers in the early stages of buying often face a confusing market. Many companies make sustainability claims that are not clear. This lack of transparency has both good and bad sides for buyers.

On the positive side, stricter rules help brands improve. Regulators now demand better proof for green claims. For example, the European Union’s Green Claims Directive requires companies to use scientific evidence. This raises the standard for honesty in the market. It protects buyers from fake marketing. It also encourages real innovation in sustainable practices.

However, these rules also create problems. Many companies fear legal trouble. So, they hide all environmental information. This is called “environmental silence.” This leaves shoppers without the data they need to compare products. A report by the Isaac Peral Foundation shows how strict rules can make firms stop talking to the public. This happens even if the company has good eco-friendly efforts. As a result, buyers feel confused. They cannot tell which brands are truly improving and which are just avoiding scrutiny.

When information is missing, it is hard to make ethical choices. Without proof, shoppers must guess. They might buy items that do not match their values. This lack of honesty hurts trust in the market. It makes it harder to support responsible businesses. To fix this, companies must balance following rules with open communication. They should share clear, evidence-based facts about their environmental impact. Only then can consumers confidently choose options that help the planet. The Isaac Peral Foundation has launched a pioneering guide designed to combat greenwashing, a deceptive practice that undermines corporate environmental credibility. Its primary objective is to help companies identify and avoid "eco-posturing," promoting rigorous communication based on verifiable data. This approach aims to protect consumers from vague or exaggerated claims, ensuring that environmental messages are transparent, specific, and backed by concrete evidence. It is crucial to recognize that greenwashing has moved beyond reputational concerns to become a legal risk with direct legal consequences. Emerging regulations, such as the European Directive on Consumer Empowerment, strictly regulate pre- and post-sale communications, penalizing unfair commercial practices. Against this legal backdrop, the article highlights that regulatory pressure can lead to both greater rigidity and "environmental silence," where companies fear communicating their genuine progress due to concerns about making mistakes or facing sanctions, thereby hindering authentic ecological transition. The significance of this text lies in its proposal to transform regulation into an opportunity to professionalize sustainability. By emphasizing that action must precede communication, the guide urges companies to act first, measure afterward, and communicate only what can be demonstrated. This ethical stance not only preserves consumer trust, built over years, but also serves as a practical tool for aligning marketing with environmental reality, avoiding the widespread distrust generated by years of misleading advertising practices.
