We can achieve a lot of nature in our fine country

The article critiques corporate rewilding initiatives that prioritize attracting investors through exaggerated environmental claims, effectively engaging in greenwashing while neglecting local communities and traditional livelihoods. It highlights how the rush to capitalize on carbon markets often sidelines the genuine needs of rural populations, creating a stark contrast between performative ecological restoration and sustainable, community-integrated practices. Ruaridh argues that true climate action should involve collaborating with indigenous and native agriculturalists rather than displacing them. By focusing on ethical investments that respect local culture and history, the sector can achieve authentic nature restoration. This approach ensures that net-zero goals are met without erasing the cultural identity and economic stability of rural residents, fostering a more inclusive definition of sustainability. This perspective is crucial for understanding greenwashing because it exposes how large-scale conservation projects can mask social inequity and cultural erasure under the guise of environmental benefit. It warns that without genuine engagement with local stakeholders, corporate-led rewilding risks becoming a superficial marketing tool rather than a meaningful solution for climate and biodiversity crises, ultimately undermining the integrity of green investments.
