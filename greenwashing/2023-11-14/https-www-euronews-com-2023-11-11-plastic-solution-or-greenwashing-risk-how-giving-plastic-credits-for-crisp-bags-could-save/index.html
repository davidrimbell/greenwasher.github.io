Greenwashing
ES EN

The article examines the emerging market for plastic credits, which allow companies to fund waste collection in underserved areas as an alternative to reducing their own plastic usage. By monetizing low-value plastics that are too expensive to recycle, this model aims to support informal waste pickers and manage pollution where traditional infrastructure fails. However, it represents a shift from prevention to financial compensation for existing waste, mirroring the offsetting mechanisms often criticized in environmental markets. Critics argue that these credits risk enabling greenwashing by allowing corporations to claim "plastic neutrality" without addressing the root causes of pollution or their supply chains. Unlike carbon offsets, the localized nature of plastic pollution makes it difficult to prove that removal in one area truly offsets impact elsewhere, while the lack of global standards raises concerns about accountability. There is a fear that this market encourages businesses to maintain high production levels and business-as-usual practices, treating the symptom rather than the disease. Ultimately, the article concludes that plastic credits should only serve as a short-term, regulated bridge within a broader strategy focused on extended producer responsibility. Genuine sustainability requires drastically reducing production and shifting away from single-use models, rather than relying on voluntary compensation schemes. Without strict regulation and a primary focus on prevention, these credits may inadvertently perpetuate the very pollution they aim to mitigate, highlighting the danger of mistaking funding for actual environmental stewardship.
