NSW has passed a special law for one coal mine. Here’s why that’s worrying

The New South Wales government passed legislation specifically enabling the expansion of the Mount Pleasant coal mine, effectively overriding a recent High Court ruling that emphasized the necessity of addressing emissions, including those from overseas consumption. This legislative intervention prioritizes short-term employment security over legal precedent, allowing the mine to operate at increased capacity until 2032. By bypassing the judicial process that was meant to enforce stricter environmental considerations, the state has signaled that political expediency can supersede established climate accountability mechanisms. Crucially, the law grants the resources minister unprecedented power to modify or disapply almost any other NSW law regarding the mine, including environmental and workplace safety regulations. This "Henry VIII clause" removes standard checks and balances, creating a significant risk of regulatory capture and corruption. Furthermore, the act attempts to restrict legal challenges to the mine’s approval, concentrating decision-making authority in the hands of the executive branch rather than maintaining judicial oversight. This erosion of democratic and legal safeguards highlights a concerning shift toward opaque, executive-dominated governance in environmental matters. This case serves as a dangerous precedent for greenwashing in climate action, demonstrating how governments can legally rebrand fossil fuel expansion as necessary economic policy while dismantling environmental protections. It reveals a pattern where legislative tools are weaponized to legitimize harmful industries, undermining public trust and climate targets. For the global climate movement, this illustrates the urgent need to scrutinize not just corporate claims, but also state-level legal frameworks that facilitate continued fossil fuel dependency under the guise of statutory necessity.
