Explained: Carbon credits

The article highlights the growing controversy surrounding carbon credit markets, which allow entities to offset emissions by funding projects elsewhere. Despite the lack of robust international regulation, this multibillion-dollar market has expanded rapidly, often enabling industries with poor environmental records to bypass genuine decarbonization efforts. This disconnect between claimed reductions and actual climate impact is central to the concept of greenwashing, where financial instruments mask a failure to reduce greenhouse gases at the source. Critics argue that many current offset mechanisms, particularly those involving fossil fuel extraction and certain forest preservation schemes, are fundamentally flawed and sometimes counterproductive. For instance, carbon capture technologies are frequently used to extract more oil and gas, while forest offsets often fail to prove that the protected land would have otherwise been destroyed. These systemic failures allow companies to purchase credits that do not represent real, additional, or permanent emissions reductions, thereby perpetuating harmful practices under the guise of sustainability. To combat this greenwashing, experts emphasize the need for strict regulatory standards based on criteria such as additionality, verifiability, and immediacy. Only offsets that provide measurable, immediate benefits without substituting for direct emission cuts should be considered legitimate. Without enforceable oversight, the carbon market remains a "wild west" prone to fraud, making rigorous evaluation essential to ensure that climate solutions actually contribute to global mitigation goals rather than serving as superficial public relations tools.
