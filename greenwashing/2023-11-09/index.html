'We cannot afford greenwashing': UN Chief Guterres highlights key role of Net-Zero experts
The UN Secretary-General established a Net-Zero Expert Group to combat the prevalent greenwashing phenomenon, addressing the severe deficit of credibility and confusion surrounding corporate climate pledges. By targeting non-State entities like businesses and investors, the initiative seeks to establish rigorous, transparent standards that prevent fossil fuel interests from exploiting geopolitical crises to maintain high carbon futures. Central to this effort is the demand for ambitious, verifiable commitments that align with environmental integrity and transparency. The group aims to replace vague promises with actionable, accountable measures, ensuring that net-zero targets are not merely marketing tools but genuine pathways to decarbonization. This focus on verification and robust reporting is essential to distinguish sincere climate action from deceptive practices. Ultimately, these recommendations intend to translate high-level standards into concrete international and national regulations. By creating a unified framework for assessing and implementing net-zero goals, the UN hopes to eliminate fake movers and accelerate global emissions reductions. This structural clarity is vital for maintaining the urgency required to keep the 1.5-degree climate target alive and ensuring genuine progress over performative activism.

Are companies really reducing waste with plastic credits, or just greenwashing? | KrASIA
The article critiques the emerging market for plastic credits, which allow corporations to offset their plastic production by funding waste collection in developing nations. With no global regulatory standards ensuring data accuracy or proper waste disposal, these systems enable companies to claim pollution neutrality while continuing to manufacture single-use plastics. This mechanism shifts the burden of cleanup onto poorer regions lacking infrastructure, creating a dynamic where financial incentives prioritize removal over prevention. Critics argue this model is fundamentally flawed because it addresses only downstream waste rather than reducing plastic production at the source. By allowing companies to purchase credits instead of redesigning products or using alternatives, the system perpetuates the exponential growth of plastic pollution. Furthermore, the lack of consistent verification raises doubts about the environmental impact of these projects, echoing the failures of carbon credit schemes that were often found to be ineffective or misleading. This topic is critically relevant to greenwashing because it provides corporations with a veneer of environmental responsibility without committing to substantive behavioral changes. Plastic credits offer a PR tool to appear sustainable while avoiding the difficult work of reduction and recycling innovation. Consequently, this approach risks distracting from necessary policies like production caps and extended producer responsibility, ultimately serving corporate interests rather than solving the global plastic crisis.

Carbon capture: between technological challenges and greenwashing concerns
The article highlights a debate surrounding Direct Air Capture (DAC) technologies, exemplified by Heirloom Carbon, which claims to offer a viable solution for reversing climate change by actively removing legacy CO2 from the atmosphere. By mimicking natural mineral processes to store carbon underground, proponents argue this technology is essential for achieving net-zero goals, especially as conventional emission reductions prove insufficient. This perspective positions carbon removal not merely as an offset mechanism, but as a critical tool for cleaning up historical emissions that have already accumulated in the air. However, significant skepticism exists regarding the efficiency and necessity of capturing dispersed atmospheric carbon versus concentrated industrial emissions. Critics point out that extracting CO2 from the ambient air is technically challenging and energy-intensive compared to capturing it at the source. This tension raises questions about whether investing heavily in DAC distracts from more immediate and effective strategies, such as drastically reducing fossil fuel consumption, thereby potentially enabling continued high-emission practices under the guise of future compensation. Relevance to greenwashing lies in the accusation that industries, particularly oil and gas, exploit carbon removal narratives to justify continued pollution. While Heirloom’s leadership explicitly condemns such tactics and commits to permanent storage, the broader industry trend risks using vague promises of future technology as a distraction from current accountability. The article underscores the danger of viewing carbon capture as an "easy ticket" to avoid systemic changes, warning that it can serve as a potent tool for greenwashing if it allows polluters to maintain the status quo while marketing themselves as climate leaders.

The EU and greenwashing regulations
The European Union has introduced stringent regulations to combat greenwashing by banning vague environmental claims and requiring substantive evidence for any sustainability assertions. This regulatory shift aims to empower consumers with transparent, accurate information, effectively eliminating deceptive marketing tactics that obscure a product’s true environmental impact. By mandating proof for terms like "eco-friendly" or "climate-neutral," the EU ensures that marketing aligns with genuine performance rather than mere perception. For the financial services sector, these rules necessitate a rigorous recalibration of due diligence processes, as institutions must now validate the environmental claims of their clients to ensure investment integrity. This requirement reinforces the credibility of sustainable investments and promotes ethical capital allocation, directly supporting the broader shift toward responsible ESG practices. Financial professionals are thus positioned as gatekeepers who must verify data to prevent capital from supporting misleading sustainability narratives. The accounting profession faces a significant transformation, requiring auditors to conduct more thorough examinations of sustainability reports and collaborate with experts to validate environmental data. This evolution establishes accountants as crucial defenders of market integrity, ensuring that corporate disclosures accurately reflect environmental realities. These measures are highly relevant to greenwashing discussions as they create a legally enforced framework that holds corporations accountable, thereby reducing the prevalence of deceptive practices and fostering a culture of authentic transparency across global markets.
