Twelve countries push carbon credits at COP31: Buying certificates is not enough to decarbonize

The initiative presented at COP31 aims to mobilize massive private investment toward climate projects by boosting demand for carbon credits. However, this commercial growth carries the inherent risk of fueling greenwashing if transaction volume is prioritized over genuine environmental effectiveness. The proposal insists that market expansion must translate solely into demonstrable climate benefits, avoiding the conflation of commercial success with actual emission reductions. It is essential to establish clear boundaries where ethical integrity and active reduction of the carbon footprint are prioritized. Purchasing certificates does not exempt companies from decarbonizing their operations; therefore, the purchase of credits must not replace the necessary investments required to eliminate pollutants. Without strict controls that guarantee the veracity of each avoided ton of emissions and protect the rights of local communities, the market risks becoming a tool for eco-posturing that legitimizes the continuation of polluting activities. The relevance of this article lies in the urgent need to verify the effectiveness of market mechanisms to prevent false environmental achievements. With studies indicating that a significant minority of current credits do not correspond to real reductions, the lack of independent audits and transparent methods undermines global climate credibility. Promoting financing without strengthening these controls would exacerbate public distrust, turning climate policy into mere empty institutional propaganda rather than a genuine solution to the ecological crisis.
