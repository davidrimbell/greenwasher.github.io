Controversial carbon credits flood COP28, yet still no rules

The article highlights the urgent danger of systemic greenwashing as major corporations and governments rush to promote carbon credit schemes ahead of established regulatory frameworks. Despite widespread scientific evidence that many current credits significantly overestimate emission reductions or fail to deliver actual climate benefits, powerful entities continue to market these instruments as "high integrity" solutions. This premature scaling suggests that credits are being used as a convenient excuse to maintain business-as-usual pollution levels rather than forcing genuine operational changes. Environmental advocates criticize this trend as a distraction tactic, often described as "smoke and mirrors," which obscures the lack of substantial climate finance and real ambition. By allowing companies to purchase offsets for projects with dubious verifiability, such as certain forest protection initiatives, the market risks legitimizing inaction. The core implication is that without strict verification, these credits function not as tools for climate mitigation, but as marketing devices that allow polluters to claim environmental responsibility without addressing the root cause of their emissions. Ultimately, the relevance to greenwashing lies in the urgent need for a common, transparent regulatory framework under the Paris Agreement to prevent this market from becoming a haven for risky practices. The absence of unified rules creates a vacuum where vague standards enable widespread abuse, prioritizing financial transactions over actual atmospheric carbon reduction. This situation underscores the critical importance of rigorous oversight to ensure that carbon markets serve as a complementary tool for real decarbonization, rather than a loophole for perpetuating environmental deception.
