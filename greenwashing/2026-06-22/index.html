EU and Taiwan Rules: Balancing Green Claims with Small Business Costs

People see many different environmental claims every day. This makes shoppers unsure when they are looking to buy things. This uncertainty is both good and bad for buyers who want real sustainability.

The European Union now has new rules. Companies must show scientific proof for their green claims. This change helps consumers. It reduces the use of vague marketing words. A 2021 study by the European Commission found that 40% of green claims were misleading or too strong. By requiring proof, the EU helps shoppers tell the difference between truly eco-friendly products and those with fake branding. This transparency lets buyers make choices that match their values.

However, strict rules also create problems. They can affect what products are available and how much they cost. Small exporters in places like Sri Lanka struggle with the high cost of EU certification. Research from the World Bank in 2023 shows that these costs can be more than 10% of the export value for small businesses. These costs often lead to higher prices for shoppers or fewer choices in stores. Buyers might find that truly sustainable products from small producers become harder to buy because of these rules.

Taiwan takes a different approach. It balances strict rules with practical steps. The government delays the need for companies to report on Scope 3 emissions. This reduces the burden on businesses. At the same time, Taiwan introduces rules to stop greenwashing. This ensures that claims are accurate. This two-part strategy aims to keep companies in the market while building trust. A 2022 report by the Taiwan Environmental Protection Administration notes that this method helps companies focus on cutting emissions, not just paperwork. For consumers, this means a more stable market with verified claims. However, the long-term effect on small businesses is still being discussed.

These regulatory changes are shaking up the green economy. Consumers get clearer information but may face higher costs or fewer options. The EU’s strict rules protect against lies. Taiwan’s careful approach tries to keep businesses involved. Both models aim to replace marketing hype with real evidence. Shoppers must weigh the value of verified sustainability against the reality of supply chains. This changing world requires buyers to look closely if they want to support true environmental progress.
