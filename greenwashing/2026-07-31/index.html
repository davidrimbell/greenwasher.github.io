ASOBIO crea un canal contra el greenwashing en los alimentos ecológicos para proteger al consumidor
The growing demand for organic food has created fertile ground for greenwashing, a practice in which companies use deceptive marketing to project an image of sustainability without adhering to actual environmental standards. This misleading approach undermines consumers’ right to make informed decisions and can harm both public health and the environment by promoting non-sustainable agricultural practices under the guise of eco-friendliness. To combat this issue, ASOBIO has launched a dedicated channel for reporting fraudulent claims, including the misuse of the European Union organic logo or the use of misleading terms such as “bio” or “organic.” This initiative aims to protect consumers from disinformation and safeguard legitimate businesses that comply with rigorous European regulations from unfair competition driven by such deceptive strategies. This effort is essential for preserving the integrity of the organic sector. By empowering the public to identify and report irregularities, the channel strengthens transparency and trust. Ultimately, it ensures that only products with official certification receive recognition, thereby protecting the credibility of genuinely sustainable products from those that merely exploit consumers’ environmental concerns for commercial gain.

Demandan a Amazon por presunto greenwashing
A lawsuit alleges that Amazon misleads consumers regarding the environmental benefits of seafood sold on its platform, a practice known as greenwashing. Shoppers claim that labels such as “sustainable” or “responsibly sourced” create a false impression of minimal ecological harm. This deceptive marketing relies on vague sustainability claims while withholding critical information about the actual origins of the products. The lawsuit highlights that many fishing vessels remain untracked, with some deliberately disabling tracking devices to conceal their locations. As a result, a significant portion of imported wild-caught seafood may not come from responsible sources. Amazon is accused of marketing these items without providing the necessary transparency to prevent consumer deception. This case is relevant to greenwashing because it illustrates how large retailers can exploit ambiguous environmental terminology. By using broad, positive labels without substantiation, companies can attract eco-conscious buyers while masking unsustainable practices. It underscores the difficulty consumers face in verifying the authenticity of sustainability claims within complex supply chains.

The challenge to California’s Truth in Recycling Act centers on whether requiring claims to reflect actual local recycling infrastructure, rather than just technical capability, violates the First Amendment. This case is critical to understanding greenwashing because it tests the legal limits of governments in preventing misleading sustainability claims. A ruling against the law could significantly weaken state efforts to ensure advertising accurately reflects real-world environmental impacts. Parallel developments in Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) and PFAS regulations further illustrate the complex compliance landscape. Industry groups are mounting constitutional challenges against EPR programs, arguing they impose excessive burdens on interstate commerce, while environmental groups scrutinize the inclusion of controversial technologies like chemical recycling. These conflicts demonstrate how legislative attempts to hold producers accountable for end-of-life management are increasingly met with legal resistance, complicating the enforcement of transparent supply chain practices. The relevance to greenwashing lies in the shifting definition of truthful environmental communication. As states move from voluntary guidelines to mandatory, multi-factor standards for recyclability and chemical disclosures, the legal system is being asked to define what constitutes “truth” in eco-marketing. Successful challenges may create loopholes allowing companies to continue making broad recyclability claims without proving local waste infrastructure can handle them, potentially perpetuating deceptive practices.
