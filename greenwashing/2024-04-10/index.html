ExxonMobil’s recent climate reports utilize visually sophisticated but scientifically hollow graphics to create a false impression of environmental responsibility. By omitting specific numerical values, clear units of measurement, and confidence intervals, the company obscures the lack of concrete progress. This deliberate ambiguity prevents readers from evaluating the actual scale or validity of the claimed emissions reductions, transforming complex data into misleading aesthetic tools rather than honest scientific communication. The analysis highlights that Exxon relies on undefined metrics, such as vague "emissions intensity" and unmeasured future technologies like carbon capture, to mask continued fossil fuel dependence. Unlike rigorous scientific visualizations that contextualize data and uncertainty, these graphs function as disinformation devices. They prioritize brand image over factual transparency, exploiting the audience’s limited scientific literacy to suggest significant action where none substantively exists. This case is critically relevant to greenwashing as it demonstrates how aesthetic design can substitute for data integrity. The company’s strategy exemplifies how major polluters manufacture the appearance of sustainability through "slick, meaningless graphics" rather than meaningful decarbonization. It underscores that greenwashing is not just about false claims, but about the structural manipulation of information to deflect scrutiny and maintain public trust while avoiding genuine climate action.

Iberdrola has filed a lawsuit for unfair competition against Repsol, alleging that its advertising constitutes greenwashing. The utility company accuses its competitor of promoting sustainable initiatives while its core business continues to rely on fossil fuels, labeling these campaigns as “eco-posturing.” This legal action aims to expose the discrepancy between Repsol’s environmental rhetoric and the reality of its business model, which is based on polluting fuels. The conflict is significant for understanding greenwashing, as it demonstrates how companies use sustainability narratives to gain market share, potentially misleading consumers and eroding overall trust in ecological claims. Iberdrola argues that such unfair practices undermine the genuine efforts of other companies that truly prioritize the energy transition, creating a distorted market where green marketing overshadows real environmental action. The lawsuit demands an end to these practices and the destruction of advertising materials, highlighting the seriousness with which the company views this communicative manipulation. Finally, the case illustrates the growing judicialization of environmental claims in the energy sector. While Repsol defends its multi-energy strategy and denies the charges, the existence of this controversy reflects the competitive tension between different visions of decarbonization. The incident is set against a broader regulatory context, such as the new European Directive against greenwashing, which aims to protect consumers from misleading commercial information, indicating that such legal disputes will become increasingly common in defining the ethical boundaries of sustainable marketing.
