Consumer NZ finds supermarket aisles awash with dubious green claims | infonews.co.nz New Zealand News
A recent investigation by Consumer NZ reveals that greenwashing is widespread among supermarket products in Aotearoa, with nearly every item examined featuring unsubstantiated or misleading environmental claims. The study highlights how vague terminology, such as "planet conscious" or "compostable," allows manufacturers to insinuate eco-friendliness without delivering tangible benefits, often charging premium prices to conscious shoppers. This practice exploits the lack of regulatory enforcement, making it difficult for consumers to distinguish genuine sustainability efforts from marketing spin. The article underscores the critical disconnect between labeling claims and real-world infrastructure, particularly regarding compostable materials. Many products labeled as biodegradable or industrially compostable are impractical for disposal in New Zealand due to the scarcity of appropriate facilities, leading consumers to inadvertently contribute to landfill waste. Additionally, terms like "ocean plastic" are shown to be technically accurate yet misleading, as they often refer to coastal runoff rather than recycled marine debris, further confusing buyers who believe they are making superior environmental choices. This investigation is highly relevant to greenwashing because it demonstrates the systemic failure to protect consumers from deceptive environmental marketing. By exposing how easy it is to use meaningless terms that lack scientific backing or practical application, the article argues for urgent regulatory intervention. It calls for stricter, proactive laws similar to those proposed in the European Union, rather than relying on individual complaints, to ensure transparency and prevent companies from capitalizing on sustainability concerns without accountability.

Greenwash vs Greenhush: Striking the right balance with your sustainability comms - edie
The article argues that while modern consumers, particularly Generation Z, are increasingly demanding environmental accountability, the frequent labeling of vague marketing claims as "greenwashing" can inadvertently stifle progress by encouraging organizations to remain silent about their sustainability efforts. This fear of scrutiny leads to "greenhushing," where companies hide their targets and achievements rather than engage in potentially criticized dialogue, ultimately slowing the pace of positive change. To accelerate meaningful sustainability, the author advocates for replacing dismissive criticism with inclusive, constructive conversations that allow organizations to discuss both their successes and challenges. By fostering an environment of compassion and understanding diverse perspectives, stakeholders can create pathways for improvement rather than penalizing early or imperfect steps. This approach recognizes that collaboration and transparency are essential ingredients for achieving significant environmental impact, rather than relying solely on punitive measures against perceived deception. Practically, the article suggests that organizations must balance honest communication with credible evidence to avoid greenwashing without falling into greenhushing. This involves aligning marketing and sustainability teams to ensure claims are backed by legitimate data and independent certifications, while openly disclosing trade-offs and ongoing challenges. By prioritizing transparency and providing clear alternative pathways for impact, businesses can build trust with increasingly savvy consumers and contribute to a more sustainable future.

World’s biggest seller of carbon offsets accused of being a scam
The article argues that the carbon offset industry is fundamentally a deceptive mechanism allowing corporations to maintain high pollution levels by purchasing imaginary environmental credits. Rather than reducing actual emissions, companies use these offsets to create a false narrative of sustainability, effectively outsourcing their responsibility while continuing business as usual. This practice shifts the financial burden onto consumers and working-class drivers, who inadvertently subsidize the very industries driving climate change. A primary example of this failure is the South Pole’s involvement in the Kariba forest project, which was vastly overestimated in its conservation impact. Consequently, major global brands purchased credits that provided negligible atmospheric benefits, meaning their claims of fighting climate change were largely unfounded. Furthermore, the vast majority of the revenue generated by these projects flowed to the offset sellers rather than the local communities tasked with preservation, highlighting a disconnect between corporate greenwashing and genuine environmental or social aid. This revelation is critical to understanding greenwashing because it exposes how the entire market relies on artificial valuation rather than tangible ecological results. The industry functions like a speculative bubble, where confidence is maintained by mandates rather than reality, allowing elites to deflect criticism through costly, ineffective gestures. By treating environmental virtue as a tradable commodity that often does not exist, the system undermines serious climate action and serves primarily as a lucrative shield for continued fossil fuel consumption.
