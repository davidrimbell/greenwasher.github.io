How Greenwashing Tricks Shoppers and Costs You More

Stories selected and summarised by AI, not reviewed by a person. Every source is linked.

Shoppers in the early stages of buying often face greenwashing. This happens when brands use false environmental claims to sell products. This practice has good and bad sides for buyers who want sustainable options. Companies benefit because they can boost sales and loyalty. They do this without making real changes to how they operate.

A study by the University of Texas at Austin found that people think green products are better quality. This is true even if there is no real difference. Because of this belief, companies can charge more money. They keep using the same old, standard ways to make products. The Federal Trade Commission’s Green Guides point out that vague words like “eco-friendly” trick shoppers. This lets brands win customers based on perception, not actual performance.

However, shoppers lose out when they fall for these tricks. They pay higher prices for items that do not help the environment. Look at Apple’s removal of iPhone power adapters as an example. The company said this change saved the environment. But buyers had to buy new chargers that did not work with their old phones. This created more waste and cost buyers more money. The actual drop in electronic waste was tiny. This shows the company cared more about profit than real impact.

Vague company goals also hurt consumers. They hide the true cost of buying these products. JBS USA settled allegations with the New York Attorney General after admitting its net-zero claims were misleading. The company changed its 2040 target from a firm promise to just a goal. It admitted it had no real plan to do this. This shows how empty promises trick buyers. People support brands that delay real action. When companies use hopeful words without solid plans, consumers lose trust in sustainability labels.

Government actions also make things harder for careful shoppers. Mark Carney’s administration dismantled anti-greenwashing regulations to help oil and gas companies compete. The government brought back tax breaks for natural gas and carbon capture. It tried to make fossil fuels look like the green choice. This move weakens national environmental goals. It suggests that money matters more than climate health. Shoppers cannot trust government certifications when policies favor fossil fuels.

The fast fashion industry is a great example of these lies. The Italian Competition Authority fined Shein one million euros for deceptive marketing. This was about its “evoluSHEIN” collection. Regulators found that claims about recycling and sustainable fibers were unclear. These efforts made up only a tiny part of Shein’s huge inventory. This ruling shows how companies exaggerate small benefits. They ignore their larger, harmful impacts. Consumers face more pollution and waste when brands use vague words to hide big problems.

Catherine McKenna, former Canadian environment minister and chair of the UN’s expert group on greenwashing, warns that climate change gets worse because of this lack of honesty. She says we must hold companies accountable. Brands that hide behind greenwashing delay urgent action. This creates a world that is harder for future generations. Shoppers must look past labels like biodegradable or renewable. Demand proof. Support rules that hold corporations accountable. This helps consumers navigate these challenges. Rejecting superficial changes allows buyers to support genuine, transparent, and ethical action.
