Greenwashing
ES EN

The article argues that while Olympic environmental initiatives often appear as greenwashing due to their temporary or incomplete nature, they serve as crucial catalysts for lasting ecological improvements. The recent cleanup of the Seine River, which failed to remain consistently swimmable despite massive investment, exemplifies this paradox. Rather than dismissing these efforts as mere deception, the narrative suggests that the political and financial momentum generated by the Games forces cities to undertake environmental projects that might otherwise never occur. Historically, the Olympics have shifted from hosting destructive infrastructure to becoming a platform for sustainability commitments. Although previous games like Beijing 2008 saw pollution levels rebound after the event, the temporary success demonstrated that environmental problems were solvable. This visibility galvanized public pressure and led to permanent policy changes, proving that even flawed green promises can accelerate necessary long-term strategies by establishing benchmarks and proving technical feasibility. Ultimately, the relevance to greenwashing lies in recognizing that "something is better than nothing." While activists may critique the inconsistency of these mega-projects, the raised public expectations and established infrastructure create a legacy that outlasts the celebrations. Future host cities may need to engage in similar strategic exaggeration to secure funding, suggesting that imperfect environmental gestures, driven by global scrutiny, can yield tangible, long-term benefits for urban environments.
