Ships of death: This notorious industry is about to change, but is it greenwashing?

The article examines the impending enforcement of the Hong Kong International Convention, which aims to mandate safer and environmentally sound ship recycling in Bangladesh. This regulatory shift threatens to idle the majority of Chittagong’s beach scrapyards, potentially displacing tens of thousands of workers who rely on this hazardous industry for survival. While the treaty introduces "green" certification requirements, critics argue that the transition will primarily harm vulnerable laborers without fully resolving the systemic dangers inherent in beach-based dismantling. Central to the greenwashing critique is the argument that the Hong Kong Convention fails to address the root causes of pollution and exploitation, such as the use of "cash buyers" and flags of convenience. These mechanisms allow shipping companies from developed nations to obscure ownership and bypass stricter Basel Convention regulations, effectively shifting the burden of toxic waste disposal onto importing states like Bangladesh. Consequently, the treaty is portrayed as a loophole that protects the interests of wealthy shipowners while leaving developing countries to manage the severe environmental and health consequences. The relevance to greenwashing lies in the disparity between the treaty’s intended safety standards and the reality of on-the-ground implementation. With Bangladesh lacking specialized hazardous waste facilities, even compliant yards resort to entombing toxins rather than disposing of them properly. This scenario illustrates how international agreements can create an illusion of environmental responsibility without ensuring actual safety, allowing industries to claim compliance while continuing to expose workers and ecosystems to significant harm.
