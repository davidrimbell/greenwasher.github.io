How Eco-Terms Hide Inaction: Protecting Power Over Planet

The Illusion of Progress: Why Your “Eco-Friendly” Choices Are Being Hijacked

We have spent decades teaching our children to care for the planet. We sorted recycling. We chose clean energy. We bought products labeled organic and sustainable. We hoped to leave a world that is healthy and strong. We believed that if we made good choices, the system would change. Today, a troubling reality is emerging. The language of environmental responsibility is being used as a weapon. It is no longer just about marketing. It is about politics and power. Two recent events show how the words we trust—green, sustainable, ethical—are losing their meaning. These words are being used to protect the way things are now.

First, we must look at high-level politics. Former UK Prime Minister Tony Blair recently suggested slowing down the end of fossil fuels. He calls this “pragmatic realism.” On the surface, this sounds like smart leadership. A slow change ensures stability. However, this approach is a form of greenwashing. By pushing for unproven technologies and delaying action, Blair gives governments and industries an excuse. They can postpone real changes to reduce carbon emissions.

The danger here is not just the delay. It is the change in the story. By calling inaction “pragmatic,” leaders pretend they care about climate goals. They protect the money-making models of oil-rich countries like the UAE and Saudi Arabia. Blair has close ties to fossil fuel companies. This suggests his influence comes from corporate friends, not science. This strategy reduces the urgency we feel as parents. It shifts the blame to citizens. It suggests we must wait for technology to improve. Meanwhile, the people causing the climate crisis continue to make money. This slow change looks like progress. In reality, it is an obstacle.

This deception does not stop in international politics. It affects our local communities and jobs. Look at the new energy storage bill in Illinois. The law supports the Climate and Equitable Jobs Act. Critics say it leaves out fair labor agreements. This excludes union jobs from Black and Brown communities. Instead, these jobs go to politically connected groups. This keeps inequality in place.

This is another form of greenwashing. It hides social exclusion behind a mask of environmental care. The projects are labeled “green.” Headlines praise renewable energy storage. Yet, true sustainability cannot exist without social fairness. By ignoring justice, policymakers support deceptive claims. When a policy is called sustainable only because it lowers carbon, but ignores who is left out, it weakens the true meaning of sustainability. It tells us that a clean environment is enough, even if it is built on an unfair foundation.

For those of us who raised our families with hope for the future, these events are sad. They show that the labels we trust—eco-friendly, renewable, ethical—are emptied of meaning by those in power. The method is the same. Leaders use the language of care to justify actions that help the powerful. Whether it is Blair’s delay of fossil fuel phase-outs or Illinois’ unfair energy bills, the result is the same. The urgency of the climate crisis is hidden behind words that protect the status quo.

But recognizing this trick is the first step to taking back our power. We must demand that “green” means more than low carbon. It must mean just, inclusive, and urgent. We must look past the fake appearance of environmental responsibility. We must ask who truly benefits from these solutions. Our children deserve a world that is not only clean but also fair. This requires us to see through the greenwashing. We must reject slow delays. We must insist that true sustainability leaves no one behind.

How ‘out of touch’ Tony Blair became a serious threat to climate action
Tony Blair’s recent policy proposals, which advocate delaying urgent fossil fuel phase-outs in favor of unproven technologies, serve as a textbook example of greenwashing by framing climate inaction as pragmatic realism. By suggesting that immediate decarbonization is economically and politically unfeasible, his arguments provide cover for governments and industries to postpone meaningful action while maintaining the appearance of engagement. This rhetorical strategy effectively dilutes the urgency required to address the climate crisis, shifting the narrative from necessary transformation to a managed slowdown. The relevance of this approach to greenwashing lies in Blair’s deep ties to petrostates, which raises concerns that his influence is shaped by the interests of fossil fuel-producing nations rather than global scientific consensus. Critics argue that his perspective is distorted by close relationships with leaders from countries like the UAE and Saudi Arabia, creating a conflict of interest where financial and geopolitical priorities override environmental integrity. This alignment suggests that the push for "pragmatic" delays is less about effective policy and more about protecting the economic models of influential oil-producing allies. Ultimately, this narrative undermines public support for legitimate climate action by dismissing lifestyle changes and immediate emissions cuts as irrational burdens. By portraying urgent decarbonization as unrealistic, elite actors can deflect responsibility onto ordinary citizens while continuing to profit from the status quo. This dynamic is dangerous because it legitimizes skepticism and delays among political elites, ultimately exacerbating the climate emergency under the guise of responsible, evidence-based leadership.
