How False Eco-Claims Trick Shoppers and Hurt the Planet

Greenwashing happens when brands lie about being green. They want to look eco-friendly, but they are not. This hurts shoppers but helps companies. Firms use these tricks to sell more items. They protect their good image without changing how they work. A study from the University of Texas at Austin shows that people think these fake green products are better. This makes shoppers loyal to the brand. It also helps the company make more money. These firms keep high profits. They do not spend money on real environmental fixes.

Shoppers lose out when they fall for these lies. They pay more for items that do not help the planet. This wastes money and misses a chance to make a real difference. Research in the Journal of Consumer Research says that trust drops fast. When buyers find out they were tricked, they stop believing all green claims. This makes it hard to find truly sustainable products later. Also, false labels can keep people buying harmful items. They think they are helping, but they are not.

Real-world examples show how this works. The case of Adani shows how big companies use UN goals. They try to make fossil fuel projects look good. This confuses consumers about the true impact. Similarly, Terra Sol Studio shows that “compostable” plastics often do not break down. They end up in landfills. This happens even with eco-friendly labels. The Forest Stewardship Council also made a mistake. It resumed certification for Asia Pacific Resources International (APP). This validates a company with a bad record. Shoppers who trust these seals get misled.

These cases show that greenwashing hides the truth. Buyers need clear info to make good choices. Words like sustainable, eco-friendly, or organic do not always mean a product is safe. Do not just trust marketing words. Look for clear data and third-party checks. Make sure your purchases match your values. Understanding this helps you see through false claims. It forces brands to be honest. This shift helps buyers avoid deception. It supports a market that rewards real care for the environment.

Adani claims its export program helps contribute to sustainable energy – but experts say that’s ‘wilful disinformation’
The article highlights a significant disconnect between Adani’s marketing and scientific consensus regarding the United Nations’ sustainable development goals. Adani claims its coal exports support global efforts for affordable and clean energy, but leading experts firmly reject this assertion. They argue that the UN goals explicitly prioritize renewable and sustainable energy sources, making the export of fossil fuels fundamentally incompatible with the agenda’s core environmental and climate action objectives. Furthermore, the piece underscores the potential for regulatory evasion, noting that Adani has paid no corporate tax despite previous promises to contribute billions to the Australian economy. This financial opacity adds another layer to the controversy, suggesting that the company’s narrative of economic benefit may be overstated. The tension between Adani’s political and economic influence in Australia and the urgent need for renewable transitions frames this as a critical issue of corporate accountability and environmental integrity. This case is highly relevant to greenwashing as it exemplifies "aggressive greenwashing," where fossil fuel interests co-opt authoritative frameworks like the UN goals to legitimize harmful activities. By labeling coal exports as sustainable, Adani attempts to mislead the public and protect its social license. This strategy dilutes the meaning of sustainability, using UN authority to obscure the reality that burning coal drives climate breakdown, thereby serving as a textbook example of how corporations distort environmental narratives to justify continued harm.

Forest Stewardship Council lifts suspension of Asia Pulp & Paper harms remedy process
The Forest Stewardship Council’s decision to resume the remediation process for Asia Pacific Resources International (APP) undermines the integrity of its ethical certification standards. By allowing a company with a history of widespread deforestation and human rights violations to proceed toward recertification before concluding necessary legal reviews, the FSC risks validating the activities of one of the world’s most destructive forestry corporations. This move prioritizes procedural speed over substantive justice, potentially exposing vulnerable communities to further harm without adequate safeguards. Environmental NGOs argue that this premature restart effectively enables significant greenwashing by permitting APP to bypass a comprehensive assessment of its extensive environmental and social legacy. Critics contend that the certification body is sidestepping its own policies and misrepresenting the concerns of local stakeholders who face ongoing land conflicts and intimidation. Consequently, the FSC’s credibility is jeopardized as it appears to facilitate a corporate narrative of rehabilitation while ignoring the scale of past harms and current controversies. This controversy highlights the systemic risks within green certification schemes when governance conflicts are not rigorously addressed. The concurrent hiring of a former FSC executive by a rival firm seeking similar certification exacerbates fears of compromised oversight and conflicting interests. Ultimately, the situation demonstrates how certification bodies can become instruments for legitimizing damaging practices, thereby misleading consumers and investors who rely on labels to ensure genuine sustainability and ethical sourcing.
