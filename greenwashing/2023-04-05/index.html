For some, greenhushing means it’s all quiet on the climate front
The article highlights how intensifying regulatory scrutiny of greenwashing is inadvertently driving a trend of "greenhushing," where companies silence their sustainability commitments to avoid legal backlash. This phenomenon emerges as authorities like ASIC and the ACCC escalate enforcement against misleading environmental claims, creating a chilling effect on corporate transparency. Consequently, major institutions are quietly retracting previously stated climate goals and removing public disclosures to minimize exposure to accusations of misleading marketing. Regulators emphasize that vague or unsubstantiated eco-claims harm both consumers and genuinely sustainable businesses by distorting market trust. The legal risk of being accused of greenwashing is so significant that firms now fear retaliation more than they value the reputational benefits of publicizing their efforts. This dynamic forces organizations to prioritize legal safety over authentic communication, resulting in a retreat from clear environmental reporting rather than an improvement in actual practices. This situation is critical to understanding greenwashing because it reveals the systemic distortion of sustainability messaging. When the cost of claiming to be green becomes legally perilous, the overall landscape of environmental accountability degrades. The article demonstrates that without safe harbors for honest disclosure, the fight against greenwashing may paradoxically reduce the visibility of legitimate corporate climate action, making it harder for stakeholders to identify truly responsible entities.

How can apparel businesses prioritise ESG?
Public confidence in Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) commitments is waning, prompting businesses to seek realistic pathways for sustainable improvement rather than pursuing unattainable perfection. Experts suggest that organizations should prioritize material risks over exhaustive assessments, identify immediate and long-term goals, and leverage internal engagement to drive responsible sourcing. By focusing efforts on the most significant areas and learning from industry peers, companies can implement sustainable practices that are both effective and resource-appropriate, thereby building genuine trust with stakeholders. The article highlights the critical danger of greenwashing, defined as making misleading or unsubstantiated claims about sustainability. To avoid reputational damage and legal penalties, businesses must rigorously scrutinize the language used in their marketing and ensure every claim is backed by concrete evidence from both their own operations and their suppliers. This transparency is essential, as consumers and regulators increasingly probe the validity of sustainability statements, requiring companies to possess robust data to substantiate their environmental and social assertions. Furthermore, companies must navigate an evolving legal landscape by mapping their supply chains and staying informed about mandatory reporting standards. Even smaller businesses are subject to these regulations, necessitating a strategic approach where firms identify trusted resources and align their planning with regulatory milestones. The core implication is that sustainability is not about addressing every global challenge but rather about selecting specific, high-impact battles where a business can make a measurable and verifiable difference.

SMH: Radio Silence Replaces Greenwashing Claims
Stricter regulations against misleading environmental claims are causing a significant shift in corporate behavior, moving the industry from active greenwashing to "green hushing." Companies are increasingly silencing their previous climate commitments to avoid legal repercussions rather than genuinely reducing their environmental impact. This trend highlights how regulatory pressure can inadvertently encourage opacity, as firms prioritize legal safety over transparency. The removal of climate reports and net-zero pledges by major entities like AustralianSuper demonstrates a retreat from public accountability, suggesting that companies may be protecting themselves from liability rather than adapting their operations. This development is crucial to understanding greenwashing because it reveals a new, less visible form of deception. By staying silent instead of lying, corporations evade scrutiny while maintaining business-as-usual practices, making it harder for consumers and regulators to assess true environmental progress and hold entities accountable for their actual ecological footprint.
