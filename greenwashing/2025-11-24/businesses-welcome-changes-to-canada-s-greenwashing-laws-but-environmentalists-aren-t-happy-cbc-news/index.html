Businesses welcome changes to Canada's greenwashing laws, but environmentalists aren't happy | CBC News

Businesses welcome changes to Canada's greenwashing laws, but environmentalists aren't happy | CBC News

Canada is amending its anti-greenwashing laws to provide greater certainty for businesses by removing vague international standards for substantiating environmental claims. While corporations have welcomed this shift as a necessary clarification, the changes raise significant concerns among environmental advocates. The primary implication is that without clear domestic benchmarks, it may become difficult to objectively judge the validity of corporate green promises, potentially undermining the rigor of previous regulations. The removal of global benchmarks creates a regulatory vacuum, leaving a lack of clear domestic standards to measure claims like net-zero emissions. This ambiguity threatens to embolden corporations to retreat from ambitious sustainability goals, as seen with major investors scaling back net-zero targets following the initial strict rules. Consequently, the market risks losing a critical mechanism for holding companies accountable, allowing potentially misleading environmental marketing to persist without stringent proof. Further weakening consumer protection is the reliance on the Competition Bureau to handle complaints, which critics argue lacks the capacity and speed to address widespread greenwashing effectively. With lengthy processing times and potential capacity issues, many grievances may go unaddressed, allowing deceptive practices to continue unchecked. This article is relevant to greenwashing because it highlights the tension between corporate convenience and genuine environmental accountability, illustrating how legislative watering-down can facilitate the very misleading claims the laws were designed to prevent.
