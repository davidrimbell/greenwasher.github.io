Automakers Should Kill Planned Obsolescence and Make Cars Last Longer

The article argues that while the European Union aims to combat planned obsolescence and greenwashing, current electric vehicle (EV) designs inherently contradict these goals. High costs and technical barriers prevent practical repairs, particularly for battery packs, forcing replacements that rival the price of new vehicles. This structural reliance on total component replacement rather than repair perpetuates a cycle of waste, undermining the environmental benefits of electrification and creating a paradox where "green" cars are not actually sustainable or repairable. Greenwashing is highlighted through the misuse of carbon neutrality claims, which the EU now prohibits unless substantiated by strict certifications. Companies often rely on vague future promises or questionable carbon credits to appear eco-friendly without making genuine operational changes. By banning unsubstantiated environmental marketing, the EU seeks to expose the discrepancy between corporate rhetoric and actual sustainability efforts, ensuring that environmental claims reflect verifiable actions rather than empty semantics or misleading narratives. Ultimately, the shift to electric mobility risks becoming a mere form of greenwashing if it preserves the logic of planned obsolescence. If EVs remain unrepairable and are replaced frequently due to prohibitively high costs, the transition fails to address the root cause of resource depletion. True sustainability requires designing products for longevity and repairability; otherwise, simply swapping internal combustion engines for batteries without changing the disposable business model amounts to no more than a superficial change that continues to harm the planet.
