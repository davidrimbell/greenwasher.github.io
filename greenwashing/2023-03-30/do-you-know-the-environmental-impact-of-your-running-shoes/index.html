Greenwashing
ES EN

Do you know the environmental impact of your running shoes?

The article exposes a critical gap between the footwear industry’s sustainability marketing and actual environmental impact, revealing that current incremental manufacturing changes are insufficient to prevent irreversible ecological damage. Despite widespread use of terms like “net-zero” and “carbon neutral,” these initiatives primarily slow the rate of growth rather than reversing harm. This disconnect highlights how corporate definitions of sustainability often prioritize the “least worst” option, failing to address the systemic overconsumption and reliance on fossil-fuel-derived materials that drive the industry’s massive carbon footprint. Relevance to greenwashing is evident in the prevalence of vague terminology and carbon offsetting schemes, which allow brands to claim climate responsibility without implementing radical supply chain transformations. The text argues that labeling products as sustainable or net-zero often serves as a marketing ploy that obscures the reality that emissions will continue to rise under existing policies. By relying on less-than-transparent offsets and recycled materials that offer only marginal emission reductions, companies create an illusion of progress while maintaining business-as-usual practices that fail to achieve the deep decarbonization required by climate science. To move beyond greenwashing, the industry must shift from incremental fixes to a truly regenerative “net positive” model that prioritizes extreme durability and reduced consumption. The authors suggest that the most significant environmental decision is simply not buying new products, urging a cultural shift away from planned obsolescence and frequent replacement cycles. True sustainability requires designing shoes for longevity, repairability, and circularity, rather than encouraging consumers to constantly purchase new items, even if they are marketed as eco-friendly alternatives.
