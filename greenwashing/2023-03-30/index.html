Automakers Should Kill Planned Obsolescence and Make Cars Last Longer
The article argues that while the European Union aims to combat planned obsolescence and greenwashing, current electric vehicle (EV) designs inherently contradict these goals. High costs and technical barriers prevent practical repairs, particularly for battery packs, forcing replacements that rival the price of new vehicles. This structural reliance on total component replacement rather than repair perpetuates a cycle of waste, undermining the environmental benefits of electrification and creating a paradox where "green" cars are not actually sustainable or repairable. Greenwashing is highlighted through the misuse of carbon neutrality claims, which the EU now prohibits unless substantiated by strict certifications. Companies often rely on vague future promises or questionable carbon credits to appear eco-friendly without making genuine operational changes. By banning unsubstantiated environmental marketing, the EU seeks to expose the discrepancy between corporate rhetoric and actual sustainability efforts, ensuring that environmental claims reflect verifiable actions rather than empty semantics or misleading narratives. Ultimately, the shift to electric mobility risks becoming a mere form of greenwashing if it preserves the logic of planned obsolescence. If EVs remain unrepairable and are replaced frequently due to prohibitively high costs, the transition fails to address the root cause of resource depletion. True sustainability requires designing products for longevity and repairability; otherwise, simply swapping internal combustion engines for batteries without changing the disposable business model amounts to no more than a superficial change that continues to harm the planet.

Consumer NZ investigates greenwashing claims
Consumer NZ’s investigation reveals that misleading environmental claims are prevalent across common supermarket products, with nearly every tested item featuring vague or unsubstantiated "green" labels. The findings highlight how manufacturers exploit ambiguous terminology to insinuate ecological benefits, effectively deceiving consumers who are increasingly mindful of sustainability. This widespread greenwashing creates an unfair market where shoppers often pay premium prices for products that offer little to no genuine environmental advantage. The analysis demonstrates that specific claims, such as "compostable" or "ocean plastic," frequently misrepresent the practical reality of waste disposal or material sourcing. For instance, items labeled as biodegradable are often impractical for New Zealanders to dispose of correctly due to a lack of industrial facilities, leading to unintended landfill use. Similarly, terms like "nature-inspired" or "planet conscious" are subjective marketing spins rather than scientific assurances, leaving consumers unable to distinguish between authentic eco-friendly innovations and superficial branding tactics. This situation is critically relevant to greenwashing because it underscores the systemic failure of current enforcement mechanisms, which rely heavily on individual consumer complaints. The investigation calls for robust regulatory intervention and pre-market verification of environmental claims, similar to measures being adopted in Australia and the European Union. Without stricter regulations, the burden remains unfairly on the public to decode complex labeling, allowing deceptive practices to persist and undermining genuine efforts toward environmental responsibility.

Do you know the environmental impact of your running shoes?
The article exposes a critical gap between the footwear industry’s sustainability marketing and actual environmental impact, revealing that current incremental manufacturing changes are insufficient to prevent irreversible ecological damage. Despite widespread use of terms like “net-zero” and “carbon neutral,” these initiatives primarily slow the rate of growth rather than reversing harm. This disconnect highlights how corporate definitions of sustainability often prioritize the “least worst” option, failing to address the systemic overconsumption and reliance on fossil-fuel-derived materials that drive the industry’s massive carbon footprint. Relevance to greenwashing is evident in the prevalence of vague terminology and carbon offsetting schemes, which allow brands to claim climate responsibility without implementing radical supply chain transformations. The text argues that labeling products as sustainable or net-zero often serves as a marketing ploy that obscures the reality that emissions will continue to rise under existing policies. By relying on less-than-transparent offsets and recycled materials that offer only marginal emission reductions, companies create an illusion of progress while maintaining business-as-usual practices that fail to achieve the deep decarbonization required by climate science. To move beyond greenwashing, the industry must shift from incremental fixes to a truly regenerative “net positive” model that prioritizes extreme durability and reduced consumption. The authors suggest that the most significant environmental decision is simply not buying new products, urging a cultural shift away from planned obsolescence and frequent replacement cycles. True sustainability requires designing shoes for longevity, repairability, and circularity, rather than encouraging consumers to constantly purchase new items, even if they are marketed as eco-friendly alternatives.
