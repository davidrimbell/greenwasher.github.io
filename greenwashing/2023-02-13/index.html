This disconnect between marketing rhetoric and operational reality suggests that firms often exploit consumer goodwill to mask their environmental footprint rather than effecting genuine change. These cases are relevant to greenwashing because they demonstrate the regulatory scrutiny applied when marketing exaggerates or fabricates ecological advantages. They underscore the importance of transparency and robust evidence in environmental claims. Greenwashing, o cómo vender más pareciendo ecológico y sustentable
The article defines greenwashing as a deceptive marketing strategy through which companies simulate environmental commitment to boost sales, exploiting legal loopholes and the lack of precise regulations. By using vague terms such as “natural” or “eco” without verifying their origin, brands create a false perception of sustainability while maintaining polluting practices, such as the excessive use of plastics within supposedly eco-friendly packaging. This disconnect between communication and operational reality allows corporations to capitalize on growing climate awareness without making substantial changes to their production chains. This practice is particularly relevant in the context of greenwashing because it diverts attention from structural environmental problems and undermines the urgency of implementing real changes. By distorting information, these strategies not only deceive consumers but also hinder the transition toward a genuinely sustainable economy. The absence of clear evaluation criteria and gaps in legal regulations enable companies to prioritize public image over environmental responsibility, generating a greater negative impact by offering superficial solutions to global crises. Finally, the text warns that the social and ethical cost of greenwashing is high, as it erodes public trust and allows companies to avoid extended producer responsibility. While some corporations genuinely integrate sustainability and inclusion, many use social and environmental causes as commercial tools to neutralize consumer pressure without addressing their carbon footprints. This dynamic underscores the need for strict regulation and transparency, since selective misinformation not only damages corporate reputation but also delays the collective action necessary to address climate change.
