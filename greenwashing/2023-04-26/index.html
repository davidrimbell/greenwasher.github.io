“The legal framework does not define what greenwashing is”
The article argues that labeling the current ESG landscape as solely greenwashing is an oversimplification that ignores the complex reality faced by asset managers. While many investors perceive products as misleadingly marketed, the author contends that much of this discrepancy stems from unintentional gaps rather than deliberate deception. The core issue is not necessarily malicious intent, but rather the structural difficulties in aligning public perception with regulatory transparency standards, leading to widespread misunderstanding of what constitutes greenwashing. A significant driver of this perception is the lack of reliable data and harmonized metrics, particularly for non-liquid assets where ESG rating agencies are scarce. Because regulatory frameworks like the SFDR mandate transparency without defining strict classification thresholds, asset managers struggle to verify and communicate their sustainability commitments accurately. This ambiguity allows for varied interpretations of an asset’s "greenness," causing funds that are compliant with disclosures yet lack deep ESG integration to be unfairly scrutinized as greenwashing practices. To mitigate these risks and improve authenticity, the author advises integrating ESG strategies from the very inception of a financial product, rather than treating it as an afterthought. Asset managers must invest in internal assessment tools and collaborate with sector-specific regulatory experts to navigate the technical complexity of compliance. By prioritizing rigorous data collection and transparent early-stage planning, firms can better align their operations with regulatory expectations, thereby reducing the opacity that fuels accusations of greenwashing. This perspective is crucial for understanding greenwashing, as it shifts the focus from simple blame to the systemic challenges of data scarcity and regulatory complexity that enable misleading perceptions.

A phenomenon called greenwashing
The article highlights the growing prevalence of greenwashing, where companies label products as sustainable without genuine environmental responsibility. This practice obscures the line between truly eco-friendly items and mere marketing gimmicks, making it difficult for consumers to identify authentic sustainability amidst a market flooded with vague "green" claims. To navigate this landscape, readers are advised to prioritize third-party certifications over self-proclaimed labels. Recognized seals from independent organizations provide verified evidence that a product meets strict standards for health and environmental impact, offering a reliable shortcut to trust that generic packaging claims cannot match. Additionally, evaluating the product’s entire life cycle and remaining skeptical of misleading visual cues is crucial. Consumers should look beyond buzzwords and leafy imagery to assess actual material longevity, recyclability, and packaging, ensuring that their purchasing choices contribute to real ecological benefits rather than supporting superficial environmental branding.

La catástrofe del Rana Plaza y el greenwashing en la moda
Ten years after the Rana Plaza disaster, Greenpeace reveals that the fast fashion industry’s widespread promotion of sustainability is largely greenwashing. Although major brands market their own eco-labels and claim ethical production, their practices remain exploitative and environmentally damaging. This deceptive marketing masks the continued use of toxic chemicals, poor labor conditions, and a lack of supply chain transparency, demonstrating that current efforts are superficial rather than substantive. The core issue lies in the massive overproduction of synthetic textiles, which generates enormous waste and relies on fossil fuels. With production volumes soaring and recycling rates negligible, the industry contributes significantly to pollution in the Global South. This model prioritizes profit over planetary health, creating mountains of non-recyclable clothing that destroy ecosystems and livelihoods, thereby contradicting the environmental benefits brands claim to offer. This report is critical to understanding greenwashing because it exposes the gap between corporate rhetoric and reality. It highlights how companies use vague labels to distract consumers while avoiding responsibility for their environmental and social impact. True sustainability requires a radical shift toward durable, repairable, and second-hand models, supported by strict regulations and radical transparency, rather than mere marketing spin.
