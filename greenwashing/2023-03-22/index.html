The article reveals that consumers are frequently misled by ambiguous food labels that exploit regulatory loopholes and marketing psychology. Manufacturers often use terms like "natural," "sugar-free," or "organic" to imply health benefits or environmental responsibility, even when products remain highly processed or nutritionally poor. These strategies, known as "healthwashing" and "greenwashing," rely on the public’s tendency to trust packaging imagery and slogans without scrutinizing the actual ingredient lists or sourcing standards, creating a deceptive gap between perception and reality. Specifically, the text highlights how unregulated or loosely defined certifications allow companies to project an eco-friendly or ethical image regardless of their practices. Labels such as "free-range," "cruelty-free," and "biodynamic" often lack strict oversight, enabling factory-farmed or unsustainable products to mimic ethical branding. This manipulation extends to environmental claims, where vague phrasing suggests sustainability without proof, effectively "greenwashing" the product’s true ecological impact and distracting consumers from the industrial realities of modern agriculture. This issue is critically relevant to greenwashing because it demonstrates how environmental and health concerns are commodified through deceptive labeling. By blurring the lines between regulated standards and marketing fluff, corporations can sell products as superior based on ethical pretenses rather than factual sustainability. Understanding these tactics empowers consumers to look beyond the "halo effect" of packaging, recognizing that superficial claims often mask significant environmental harm and poor nutritional value, thereby undermining genuine efforts toward transparency and accountability.

2022 puso presión en el impulso de prácticas ESG, según informe de Thomson Reuters Institute
The “ESG Under Pressure” report by Thomson Reuters highlights that the geopolitical crisis and political polarization are exacerbating the legal and reputational risks associated with greenwashing. Companies that promote environmental or social credentials without a solid foundation are facing a perfect storm that challenges their carbon goals and demands a more rigorous social license to operate. In this context, it is crucial for organizations to carefully manage their data governance, talent development, and corporate communication strategy. Regulators, investors, and environmentalists are paying closer attention to the actual actions of companies, meaning that greenwashing is no longer a viable option given the increased scrutiny from both the public and regulators. The relevance of this analysis lies in the fact that operational alignment with ESG criteria has gone beyond mere regulatory compliance to become a strategic pillar of business. Ignoring transparency and truthfulness in sustainability claims not only jeopardizes access to capital and talent retention but also threatens the financial viability and future of companies.

Consumers who encounter greenwashing claims can report to CASE: MOS Alvin Tan
Singapore’s regulatory framework currently addresses greenwashing through existing consumer protection laws rather than specific legislation, with the Competition and Consumer Commission of Singapore monitoring global developments to assess potential updates. Authorities emphasize that suppliers must substantiate environmental claims, and consumers encountering misleading practices are encouraged to seek assistance from the Consumer Association of Singapore for resolution. Although no specific complaints have been lodged with these bodies yet, the government retains the authority to investigate egregious cases of deceptive advertising. This approach relies on broad current statutes to handle business-to-consumer transactions, ensuring that advertisers clearly explain and qualify their environmental statements without requiring immediate, rigid legislative intervention. This scenario is relevant to greenwashing as it highlights the regulatory gap between voluntary guidelines and mandatory legal penalties prevalent in other jurisdictions like the EU and UK. The absence of reported cases may indicate under-enforcement or lack of consumer awareness, underscoring the vulnerability of markets where green claims are not strictly audited, thereby facilitating deceptive marketing practices that erode public trust.
