EU Aviation Disaster: Will Von der Leyen Rubber-Stamp Biggest Aviation Greenwashing in Decades?
The article argues that proposed EU taxonomy criteria for aviation represent a massive act of greenwashing, allowing investments in fuel-efficient fossil-fuel planes to be labeled as environmentally sustainable. By classifying marginal efficiency gains as "green," the framework risks directing substantial capital toward an industry that continues to rely on polluting fossil fuels, thereby legitimizing business-as-usual operations under a false environmental banner. This approach fails to address the core issue, as historical data shows that improved fuel efficiency has not prevented exponential growth in CO2 emissions. Consequently, labeling these aircraft as sustainable misleads investors and allows major manufacturers and airlines to secure green funding without implementing genuine decarbonization strategies, effectively subsidizing the continuation of high-emission practices for the foreseeable future. The piece is relevant to greenwashing because it illustrates how regulatory standards can be manipulated to create a false narrative of sustainability. It highlights the danger of accepting incremental technical improvements as transformative solutions, urging policymakers to reject such loopholes and instead mandate funding only for technologies with zero-emission potential to ensure true climate progress.

Government urged to introduce EPR for renewable energy sector - letsrecycle.com
The renewable energy sector faces a significant risk of greenwashing if it fails to address the environmental consequences of its own infrastructure. As the industry shifts from fuel-intensive to material-intensive systems, it is generating a massive wave of waste from solar panels, wind turbines, and batteries. Without proactive measures, this surge in end-of-life equipment will exacerbate environmental problems, undermining the sector’s core mission to combat climate change and potentially rendering its "green" claims hypocritical. To mitigate this, the report advocates for a transition to circular economy models driven by industry players, investors, and policymakers. This involves designing assets for recyclability, building circular value chains, and establishing appropriate economic conditions. By extending producer responsibility and funding research into recycling technologies, the sector can reduce its reliance on critical raw materials and decrease supply risks. These strategies are essential for decoupling renewable energy growth from increased material consumption. This approach is relevant to greenwashing because it exposes the gap between the clean energy narrative and the physical reality of resource extraction and waste. Ignoring the lifecycle impacts of renewable technology allows companies to claim environmental benefits while shifting negative burdens elsewhere. Implementing circularity ensures that the green transition is genuinely sustainable, preserving the integrity of the sector and preventing it from inadvertently driving new forms of environmental degradation.

The thin green line — where disinformation meets greenwashing
The article illustrates how climate disinformation, often overlapping with greenwashing, actively undermines urgent environmental action by spreading coordinated falsehoods. Greenwashing is identified as a specific subset of this disinformation, where corporations use misleading branding to exaggerate their sustainability efforts while continuing to prioritize fossil fuel extraction. This strategic misrepresentation allows polluting industries to appear environmentally responsible without making significant changes to their core business models, effectively deceiving consumers and delaying regulatory intervention. Political figures and corporate actors leverage these tactics to shift blame onto individuals or blame policy mechanisms like carbon taxes, rather than addressing the systemic role of fossil fuels. By amplifying these narratives through social media algorithms, right-wing populist movements and major oil companies successfully erode public trust in science and government. This coordinated effort not only blocks essential emissions-reduction policies but also fuels broader right-wing populism, creating a cycle where misinformation prevents meaningful climate action. Understanding these tactics is crucial for recognizing how greenwashing and disinformation reinforce each other to maintain the status quo. The text highlights that while outright climate denial has evolved, the core strategy remains the same: confusing the public with spin, individualism, and false technological optimism. By identifying these patterns, such as the discrepancy between corporate marketing claims and actual investment in clean energy, individuals can better discern genuine sustainability efforts from manipulative greenwashing, thereby supporting policies that drive real ecological progress.
