TotalEnergies: Greenwashing en estado puro
The article concludes that TotalEnergies engages in "pure greenwashing" by preserving its core business model while promoting a false narrative of ecological responsibility. Despite rebranding and pledging substantial emission reductions, the company continues to invest heavily in large-scale new fossil fuel projects in Uganda and the United States. This contradiction reveals that the corporation prioritizes the continued extraction and sale of oil and gas over genuine environmental stewardship, using sustainability claims merely to sanitize its public image. Financial incentives remain the primary driver, as renewable energy has not yet proven sufficiently profitable compared to traditional hydrocarbons. The investigation highlights that while the company invests billions in expanding production, its actual contribution to renewable energy is negligible. Industry experts confirm that major oil companies are reluctant to fully transform their business models because fossil fuel extraction remains far more lucrative, indicating that the transition is rhetorical rather than operational. This case is crucial for understanding greenwashing because it exposes the mechanisms companies use to avoid accountability for climate change. By launching high-profile environmental initiatives while simultaneously destroying ecosystems and displacing communities through new fossil fuel projects, TotalEnergies creates a dissonance between its stated values and its actions. The article illustrates how multinational corporations leverage the urgency of the climate crisis to maintain public legitimacy without altering the harmful practices that contribute to global warming.

Vegan v real leather: the inside story that’s dividing carmakers
Manufacturers market plastic-based materials as "vegan leather," creating self-contradictory terms that mislead consumers about the product's true nature and environmental impact. While some alternatives offer high quality, many remain plasticky, obscuring the reality that these substitutes often rely on synthetic chemicals rather than genuine sustainable practices. This ambiguity exemplifies greenwashing by exploiting the ethical appeal of veganism to disguise the reliance on non-biodegradable, petroleum-derived materials in modern automotive design.

Why so much recycled clothing is still going in the garbage | CBC News
Major retail brands are misleading consumers with vague sustainability claims, particularly regarding recycled materials, which constitute a significant form of greenwashing. Investigations reveal that products labeled as eco-friendly often contain minimal recycled content or are still predominantly made from fossil fuel-based plastics. By highlighting small sustainable features while obscuring the overall environmental impact, companies distract shoppers from the reality that these items are essentially single-use plastics destined for landfills, thereby perpetuating a linear consumption model rather than a circular one. The term "recycled" is frequently used inaccurately to imply true environmental benefit, when in practice it often refers to downcycled plastic bottles that cannot be effectively turned back into new clothing. Experts argue that this marketing exploits consumer desire for sustainability, misleading them into believing they can endlessly consume new garments knowing they will be recycled. However, the technology for textile-to-textile recycling is not yet scalable, meaning most "recycled" fashion contributes to waste accumulation rather than reducing it, despite the lower carbon footprint of recycled polyester compared to virgin materials. This widespread greenwashing harms both consumers, who pay premiums for ineffective solutions, and legitimate sustainability efforts by diluting the value of authentic environmental progress. Regulatory bodies have already begun challenging these practices, noting that vague terms like "conscious" fail to explain actual sustainability benefits. As a result, companies face increasing scrutiny and promises to reform labeling, yet the discrepancy between marketing narratives and the actual recyclability of blended fibers remains a critical issue, undermining trust and slowing meaningful industry change.
