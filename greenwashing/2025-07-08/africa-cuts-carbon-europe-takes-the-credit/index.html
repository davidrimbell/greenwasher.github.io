Africa cuts carbon, Europe takes the credit

Africa cuts carbon, Europe takes the credit

The EU proposes allowing member states to count overseas carbon credits toward domestic emission targets by 2040. This policy aims to boost industrial competitiveness by leveraging financing for green projects in Africa, with credits capped at three percent of total emissions. The initiative reflects a growing reliance on international markets to meet net-zero goals, positioning carbon offsetting as a strategic tool for economic and environmental policy alignment. Critics argue this approach creates perverse incentives, potentially encouraging developing nations to maintain high emissions while selling offsets. Evidence suggests that a small minority of credits deliver genuine reductions, and some countries receiving funds continue investing in fossil fuels like coal. This discrepancy raises serious doubts about the actual environmental benefit of such transactions, highlighting the gap between marketed claims and on-the-ground realities. This development is highly relevant to greenwashing because it institutionalizes a system where wealthy nations can buy compliance credits rather than making deep domestic cuts. By allowing questionable offsets to count toward official targets, the EU may appear environmentally responsible while masking incomplete action. This risks legitimizing ineffective projects and misleading the public about the true progress toward climate neutrality.
