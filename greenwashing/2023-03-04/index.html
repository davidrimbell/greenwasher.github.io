'Greenwashing' fast fashion brands are going all in on the resale market. But consumers are pushing back - The Big Issue
Major fashion experts characterize the introduction of resale platforms by ultra-fast fashion brands as an egregious form of greenwashing. Rather than addressing the root causes of environmental harm, these initiatives serve as a "get-out-of-jail-free card," allowing companies to appear eco-conscious while continuing to encourage excessive consumption. Critics argue this strategy masks the fact that these brands are designed for disposability, making the notion of reselling their low-quality, short-lived garments fundamentally redundant and deceptive. The core issue lies in the inherent contradiction of trying to sustain a business model built on rapid, trend-driven overproduction through circular economy tactics. Industry analysts emphasize that genuine sustainability requires scaling back production and creating durable goods, not merely adding a resale feature to maintain high profit margins. By focusing on surface-level changes, these corporations exploit consumer inexperience, perpetuating a cycle of waste while profiting from the very practices that damage the planet and exploit workers. True accountability cannot be achieved through voluntary corporate initiatives alone, as the current system prioritizes profit over planetary health. The article concludes that meaningful change requires robust legislation, independent auditing, and stricter consequences for polluters. Consumers are urged to remain discerning and reject brands that fail to address the fundamental drivers of waste, recognizing that buying less and choosing quality over quantity remains the most effective individual action against the fast fashion crisis.

'Like the subprime mortgage crisis': Most of Australia's carbon credits are junk, experts say
This article exposes a critical flaw in Australia’s safeguard mechanism, where major polluters can purchase dubious carbon credits based on existing vegetation rather than actual new tree growth. Critics argue that the government’s decision to allow credits for “trees grown inside other trees” creates a massive supply of low-integrity offsets. This practice contradicts independent review recommendations intended to ensure environmental accuracy, effectively enabling companies to claim emission reductions that are largely fictitious. The situation is likened to a financial crisis driven by a lack of transparency and quality control, with experts warning that the scheme floods the market with junk assets. By refusing to limit the issuance of these questionable units, the government ensures a steady, cheap supply for heavy emitters. This economic strategy prioritizes affordable offsets for industry over genuine climate action, allowing coal and gas companies to maintain their operations while appearing compliant through paper-based abatement. This case is highly relevant to greenwashing as it illustrates how systemic regulatory failures and deliberate loopholes can legitimize false environmental claims. When authorities permit the counting of non-additional carbon sinks, they enable corporations to mask their true environmental impact. This not only undermines the integrity of national climate policies but also demonstrates how greenwashing can be institutionalized, turning a vital climate tool into a mechanism for maintaining business-as-usual emissions under the guise of sustainability.

Oil and gas companies needed to make green hydrogen a reality
The article challenges the simplistic narrative that oil and gas companies are solely climate villains, arguing that they possess critical infrastructure and expertise essential for the energy transition. While these firms have delayed clean energy adoption, they remain indispensable because hydrocarbons will likely serve as raw materials for plastics and fertilizers for decades. Consequently, a successful shift to net zero requires leveraging their established capabilities in heavy engineering and risk management rather than ignoring their role entirely. A key opportunity lies in the development of green hydrogen, a sector where oil and gas majors have a distinct advantage due to existing experience with hydrogen handling and distribution networks. The author highlights specific investments by companies like Shell and BP in renewable projects, suggesting that those who reinvent themselves as broader energy conglomerates will lead the transition. This evolution involves moving beyond fossil fuel extraction to dominate new markets in green chemicals and synthetic fuels, utilizing their global logistical networks to scale emerging technologies. This perspective is relevant to greenwashing because it reframes corporate environmental strategies from mere public relations exercises to potential operational necessities. Understanding that some major players are adapting their core business models to survive helps consumers and regulators distinguish between genuine, structural reinvestment and superficial eco-branding. It encourages a more nuanced evaluation of these corporations, recognizing that their participation might be vital for achieving global climate goals, even if their past and present practices remain deeply flawed.

Plastic producers warned against making unverified environmental claims
SANS 1728 mandates rigorous testing and certification before manufacturers can label plastics as eco-friendly, explicitly banning vague claims like "green" or "non-polluting." This standard aims to prevent misleading marketing by requiring verified proof of degradability and clear material identification codes on packaging. Currently, no products in South Africa hold official SABS certification under this standard, signaling a critical gap between marketing assertions and actual environmental compliance. Consumers are urged to remain vigilant, as unverified claims often indicate a lack of adherence to necessary scientific verification processes. This regulation is vital for combating greenwashing by establishing a clear, enforceable benchmark for environmental labeling. By aligning with global requirements and integrating with extended producer responsibility regulations, it exposes unsubstantiated claims, forcing transparency and holding companies accountable for the true environmental impact of their products.

Toyota accused of greenwashing in Greenpeace complaint filed to ACCC
Greenpeace Australia Pacific has urged the ACCC to investigate Toyota’s environmental claims, alleging they mislead consumers about the company’s commitment to sustainability. The complaint highlights a stark contradiction between Toyota’s net-zero ambitions and its actual production strategies, specifically its hesitation to rapidly adopt electric vehicles. Furthermore, Greenpeace challenges the validity of Toyota’s hybrid and hydrogen fuel cell vehicles, arguing their emissions benefits are overstated given their reliance on petrol and non-renewable energy sources. This case underscores the growing scrutiny corporate entities face regarding their environmental marketing. Regulators are increasingly aggressive in policing these claims, as evidenced by recent ACCC probes into widespread misleading statements across industries. Toyota’s defense relies on its history with hybrids and emissions-reducing technologies, yet activists argue these efforts delay the essential transition to clean transport while the company lobbies against stricter global emissions standards. The relevance to greenwashing is profound, as it illustrates how companies may leverage partial truths or outdated technologies to appear environmentally responsible without making substantive changes. Such practices risk eroding consumer trust and damaging brand reputation when exposed by watchdogs and NGOs. Ultimately, this situation exemplifies the critical need for transparency and accountability, ensuring that corporate sustainability narratives align with genuine, measurable reductions in environmental impact rather than serving as mere marketing tools.
