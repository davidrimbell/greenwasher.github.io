LankaWeb – Greenwashing Bond Scams and Financializing Mother Nature: Climate Science Fiction versus Local Realities -Part I

LankaWeb – Greenwashing Bond Scams and Financializing Mother Nature: Climate Science Fiction versus Local Realities -Part I

The article argues that Sri Lanka’s ambitious green transition initiative, backed by a $100 billion price tag, is a superficial response to global narratives that obscures deeper financial exploitation. By promoting this agenda through the lens of a supposed "climate emergency," the government is prioritizing international policy trends over local economic realities, despite the nation’s negligible carbon footprint. This approach allows foreign entities to exploit the country’s vulnerability, framing aggressive debt accumulation as environmental stewardship rather than recognizing it as a continuation of predatory lending practices. Central to this critique is the mechanism of using Environment, Social, and Governance (ESG) and "Blue" bonds to service existing sovereign debt. The author contends that these instruments represent a form of greenwashing, where private creditors like BlackRock and Adani profit from high-interest loans disguised as sustainable investments. By mandating further borrowing from the same private markets that caused the initial default, the IMF and international financial institutions deepen Sri Lanka’s debt trap. This cycle impoverishes citizens while providing lucrative returns to hedge funds that are actively rebranding themselves as environmentally responsible. This scenario is highly relevant to greenwashing as it illustrates how sustainability jargon can be weaponized to justify financial extraction and undermine national sovereignty. The article highlights how "green" labels are used to mask opaque transactions and legitimize policies that prioritize creditor interests over genuine ecological or social benefits. Ultimately, it exposes a pattern where global institutions leverage fear-based climate narratives to enforce economic control, rendering local contexts invisible and turning essential development needs into vehicles for corporate profit.
