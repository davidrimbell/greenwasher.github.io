Is greenwashing really in decline?

The article challenges the narrative that greenwashing is declining, arguing that companies are increasingly opting for "greenhushing" to avoid regulatory scrutiny and political backlash rather than improving practices. This strategic withdrawal of ESG terminology, particularly among financial institutions seeking to appease US investors, constitutes a new form of deception by withholding material information from stakeholders. Consequently, the issue is shifting from exaggerated claims to deliberate silence, maintaining misleading impressions without facing the risks associated with explicit marketing. Simultaneously, the nature of greenwashing has evolved toward more severe cases where sustainability credentials are used to mask significant environmental violations. While minor claims may be receding due to fines and new regulations like the EU’s Green Claims Directive, serious infractions involving heavy pollution are increasing. This indicates that while the volume of overt marketing has dropped, the underlying problem is deepening, with industries like aviation and energy relying heavily on dubious carbon offsets to conceal their true environmental impact. Relevance to greenwashing lies in understanding its adaptive resilience; it is not disappearing but mutating in response to political and regulatory pressures. Experts warn that sustainability is increasingly treated as a liability, leading to a "competence greenwashing" where skills are downplayed for safety. This shift highlights that the core issue remains intact: corporations continue to manipulate public perception of their environmental responsibility, now through omission and risk avoidance rather than false boasting, suggesting the greenwashing problem is far more entrenched than aggregate data suggests.
