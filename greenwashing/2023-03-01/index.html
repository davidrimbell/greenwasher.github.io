‘Should send shockwaves’: ASIC sues Mercer Super in greenwashing crackdown
The Australian Securities and Investments Commission has initiated landmark legal proceedings against Mercer Superannuation for allegedly greenwashing its investment products. This action marks a significant escalation in regulatory enforcement, targeting claims that Mercer marketed certain funds as exclusively sustainable while continuing to invest in fossil fuel, alcohol, and gambling industries. By pursuing civil penalties and injunctions, ASIC aims to compel financial institutions to ensure their marketing statements accurately reflect their actual investment practices. The relevance of this case to greenwashing lies in its demonstration of strict adherence to disclosure integrity over subjective sustainability labels. Rather than debating the precise definition of "green" investing, regulators are focusing on the fundamental requirement that any positive claim made to investors must be factually supportable. This approach exposes the disconnect between promotional imagery and actual portfolio holdings, highlighting how companies can mislead consumers who seek ethical alignment for their savings. This enforcement serves as a critical warning to the broader financial sector, signaling that greenwashing will no longer be tolerated. With increasing consumer demand for sustainable options, the risk of misleading marketing is rising, making transparency paramount. The outcome of this case is expected to set a precedent, forcing the industry to rigorously verify and implement the exclusions they promise, thereby protecting investors from deceptive practices and restoring trust in sustainable finance.

A response to Paul Krugman: Growth is not as green as you might think
This article refutes the claim that economic growth is inherently compatible with environmental sustainability, arguing that such narratives are misleading and dangerous. The author demonstrates that relying on isolated metrics, like improved air quality or localized production statistics, ignores the complex reality of ecological limits. True sustainability requires respecting planetary boundaries across all environmental pressures, a standard high-income nations currently fail to meet despite their wealth. The piece highlights how greenwashing tactics often exploit statistical ambiguities, particularly the distinction between territorial emissions and total consumption footprints. By shifting polluting industries abroad, wealthy countries appear to decouple growth from environmental harm locally, while their actual global impact remains unchanged or worse. This selective reporting creates an illusion of progress, masking the fact that high consumption levels continue to drive significant ecological degradation beyond national borders. Furthermore, the analysis reveals that recent emission reductions are frequently linked to economic downturns rather than sustainable growth policies. The argument concludes that maintaining positive GDP growth fundamentally complicates the urgent need for drastic emission cuts required by climate science. Consequently, the author suggests that true decoupling is unattainable under current growth paradigms, implying that degrowth or strict consumption limits are necessary to avoid crossing irreversible ecological thresholds.

Alberta’s War Room accuses Catherine McKenna of misleading Canadians
Catherine McKenna, the UN’s greenwashing expert, accuses Canada’s Pathways Alliance of misleading the public by claiming a net-zero trajectory while simultaneously expanding fossil fuel infrastructure and lobbying against climate policy. She argues that credible net-zero commitments require absolute emission reductions and significant investment in clean energy, rather than relying on profits returned to shareholders or taxpayer subsidies. This highlights the core greenwashing tactic of making vague long-term pledges without immediate, verifiable action to decarbonize operations. The Alberta Oil and Gas War Room counters that McKenna’s critique attempts to mislead Canadians by ignoring the industry’s plans to utilize speculative carbon capture technology. However, this defense exposes a common greenwashing vulnerability: reliance on unproven technological fixes that cannot address downstream emissions from fuel combustion. By focusing on capture during extraction rather than eliminating the use of fossil fuels, these claims create an illusion of environmental responsibility while failing to reflect the full lifecycle impact of the sector’s activities. This conflict is critical to understanding greenwashing because it underscores the necessity of rigorous, science-based standards for corporate climate claims. McKenna’s UN recommendations emphasize that credible pledges must align with limiting global warming to 1.5°C, requiring short-term targets rather than distant goals reliant on ineffective technologies. The dispute illustrates how industries may exploit ambiguous definitions of "net-zero" to maintain business-as-usual practices, urging stricter accountability to prevent deceptive narratives from obscuring the urgent need for genuine emission reductions.

Climate goals or 'greenwashing?' Truth-in-advertising group doubts JBS Net-Zero claims
The National Advertising Division has challenged JBS Foods’ claim that it will achieve net-zero greenhouse gas emissions by 2040, ruling that the company’s evidence does not support such an ambitious timeline. This case highlights a critical aspect of greenwashing: the disconnect between marketing-driven sustainability pledges and the absence of viable, scientifically grounded implementation plans. By accusing JBS of misrepresenting its capabilities, the ruling underscores how corporate claims often outpace actual operational readiness, misleading consumers into believing significant environmental progress is imminent when it may not be. This conflict is particularly relevant to greenwashing in the meat industry, where biological realities make deep decarbonization extremely difficult. Experts note that livestock emissions, primarily methane and nitrous oxide, stem from inherent biological processes rather than just industrial operations, rendering engineering solutions insufficient for achieving net-zero status. Furthermore, the lack of standardized data for these emissions allows companies to obscure the true scale of their impact. When corporations promote "net-zero" goals without accounting for these complex biological factors, they create a distorted narrative that masks the fundamental unsustainability of their core business model. The situation also exposes the ambiguity surrounding Scope 3 emissions, which account for the majority of a food company’s carbon footprint but lack universal accounting standards. Without transparent reporting mechanisms, corporations can manipulate these figures to appear more sustainable than they are, turning climate commitments into marketing tools rather than actionable strategies. This case is significant because it illustrates how the absence of strict oversight allows companies to exploit regulatory gaps. It demonstrates that greenwashing thrives not just in lies, but in the strategic use of vague, unverified claims that exploit the complexities of supply chain accounting to deflect scrutiny.

EU to crack down on greenwashing with ‘proportionate’ penalties
The European Union is introducing a strict new directive requiring companies to substantiate environmental claims with rigorous proof or face significant penalties. This legislative move directly addresses the widespread prevalence of vague, misleading, and unfounded green marketing, aiming to restore consumer trust by eliminating the current "wild west" market environment where deceptive practices thrive. A core mechanism of this regulation is the mandatory use of standardized product lifecycle analyses, with verification data made publicly accessible via digital links. By enforcing clear definitions and precise criteria for green claims, the EU seeks to harmonize the fragmented landscape of over 200 existing eco-labels, ensuring that sustainability assertions are transparent, verifiable, and grounded in comprehensive environmental impact assessments rather than superficial marketing tactics. Despite these advances, experts warn that the draft law’s silence on specific carbon offsetting methods may allow fossil fuel companies to continue relying on unreliable nature-based solutions to justify emissions. This gap highlights the complexity of combating greenwashing, as robust enforcement and clear boundaries on what constitutes legitimate climate action remain critical for preventing corporations from using ambiguous offsets to bypass genuine reductions in their carbon footprints.

Tesco accused of greenwashing over ‘biodegradable’ teabags
Researchers have formally complained to Irish consumer watchdogs against Tesco, arguing that its “biodegradable” teabags fail to break down in soil, thereby misleading customers. An academic experiment demonstrated that the PLA-based bags remained intact after a full year of burial in garden soil, contradicting the common consumer expectation that such materials would naturally decompose in an open environment. This discrepancy highlights a significant gap between marketing labels and actual environmental performance, a core issue in understanding greenwashing. Tesco defends its position by stating the packaging clearly instructs consumers to use industrial composting facilities rather than home bins or soil. However, critics argue that labeling a product as “biodegradable” implies natural breakdown, making the specific disposal requirements insufficient to justify the claim. The researchers contend that the term is being used to create an impression of sustainability that does not align with the product’s behavior in typical household settings, suggesting the label is fundamentally inaccurate for general consumer understanding. This case is highly relevant to greenwashing because it exemplifies how vague environmental terminology can manipulate eco-conscious buyers. By using “biodegradable” without ensuring compatibility with home composting, retailers may exploit the lack of standardized definitions to appear sustainable while continuing to contribute to plastic pollution. Such practices undermine trust in environmental claims and illustrate the urgent need for stricter regulations on how companies describe their products’ end-of-life impact.
