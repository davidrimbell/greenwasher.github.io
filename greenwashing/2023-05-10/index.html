Church Of England Loses Faith In Shell’s Climate Plans | OilPrice.com
The Church of England has decided to vote against the reappointment of Shell’s directors and oppose its climate strategy, citing a significant lack of progress in transitioning to renewable energy. This decisive action stems from the Church’s belief that Shell missed a critical opportunity to invest its substantial windfall profits into low-carbon solutions, instead prioritizing short-term gains and fossil fuel production. By withdrawing support for the company’s leadership and its current proposals, the Church aims to force a more aggressive alignment with global net-zero goals, signaling that financial success cannot excuse delayed environmental responsibility. This stance is highly relevant to greenwashing as it highlights the tension between corporate promises and actual investment behavior. Shell claims to be advancing its net-zero targets, yet the Church argues this narrative masks a continued reliance on oil and gas. The situation demonstrates how investor pressure can expose discrepancies between stated sustainability ambitions and strategic actions, challenging companies to back their "green" claims with tangible capital allocation rather than just rhetoric. It underscores the risk that companies may use vague transition plans to maintain social license while expanding fossil fuel operations. Ultimately, while the Church’s financial stake is minor, its activism serves as a potent symbol intended to influence broader shareholder behavior. The move suggests that traditional ethical investors are becoming less tolerant of perceived greenwashing, potentially encouraging other major funds to demand stricter accountability. This pressure could disrupt the industry norm where oil majors retain majority support despite scaling back commitments, pushing for a genuine shift away from fossil fuels toward a sustainable energy future.

Greening the MBA, not greenwashing it
Management education often prioritizes technical business disciplines over transdisciplinary approaches, leaving students ill-equipped to navigate complex, modern challenges. Integrating fields like literature fosters holistic thinking, enabling future leaders to understand human behavior and market dynamics beyond mere financial metrics. While sustainability remains a prevalent buzzword, many institutions engage in superficial adoption rather than deep structural integration. This highlights a critical greenwashing risk where environmental commitments are signaled through visible but limited actions, such as campus recycling initiatives, without fundamentally reshaping curricula or operational values to reflect genuine ecological responsibility. Furthermore, the emotional and psychological well-being of students is frequently neglected in favor of rigorous, traditional pedagogies. True leadership requires social sensitivity and emotional intelligence, which cannot be cultivated through standard, high-pressure frameworks. Accreditation bodies must also move beyond rigid standards to recognize authentic innovation, ensuring that educational practices align with broader ethical and societal needs rather than mere compliance. This article underscores the gap between stated educational values and actual practices, revealing how management schools may claim holistic, sustainable, and empathetic formation while delivering narrow, technically focused, and emotionally detached training. Such discrepancies exemplify greenwashing in education, where the appearance of comprehensive reform masks a continued reliance on outdated, exclusive, and superficial methodologies that fail to address the complex human and environmental realities of the modern business world.

Italian oil firm Eni faces lawsuit alleging early knowledge of climate crisis
This article details Italy’s first climate lawsuit against Eni, accusing the oil major of greenwashing and misleading lobbying to promote fossil fuels despite understanding their climate risks decades ago. The core of the legal action rests on internal historical reports proving that Eni’s own scientists accurately predicted the catastrophic consequences of rising carbon dioxide levels as early as the 1970s. Yet, simultaneously, the company advertised natural gas as a clean solution, demonstrating a deliberate pattern of concealing scientific truths to protect market interests. The relevance to greenwashing is profound, as this case exemplifies the strategic disinformation tactics used by fossil fuel companies to maintain public trust while expanding production. By highlighting the discrepancy between internal knowledge of climate dangers and external marketing campaigns, the lawsuit exposes how "clean" labels are often employed to deflect scrutiny and delay regulatory action. This mirrors global trends where oil giants are held accountable for prioritizing profit over transparency, turning corporate sustainability claims into tools for deception rather than genuine environmental stewardship. Ultimately, the lawsuit aims to force Eni to slash its carbon emissions significantly, leveraging similar successful precedents set against Royal Dutch Shell in the Netherlands. By including government entities that hold stakes in the company, the case seeks to establish legal accountability for both corporate deception and state complicity. This development signals a growing judicial scrutiny of the fossil fuel industry’s historical and current practices, challenging the narrative that oil companies acted responsibly or without foresight regarding the climate crisis.

Senator reports energy firms to advertising watchdog over 'greenwashing' claims
Senator Lynn Boylan has lodged formal complaints against Pinergy and SSE Airtricity with the Advertising Standards Authority of Ireland for making misleading "100% renewable energy" claims. These companies argue their electricity is fully renewable, but the reality is that Ireland’s grid relies on only a minority of renewable sources, with the majority generated by fossil fuels and other non-renewable methods. Boylan argues this constitutes textbook greenwashing because individual renewable energy purchases are mixed with non-renewable power within the national grid before reaching consumers. Consequently, providers cannot truthfully claim their electricity is entirely sustainable, as customers receive a blended supply rather than exclusively green power, regardless of the supplier’s procurement strategy. This case is highly relevant to greenwashing because it tests the enforcement of precedents set by previous regulatory rulings. The Advertising Standards Authority previously banned similar claims against another provider, Energia. By holding these companies accountable, the authority demonstrates whether its industry-wide standards are effective or if companies can continue to exploit ambiguous definitions to mislead consumers about their environmental impact.
