Former fossil fuel engineer crusades against gas company greenwashing
The article argues that the fossil fuel industry successfully masks the environmental dangers of methane through a long-standing greenwashing strategy centered on nomenclature. By branding the greenhouse gas as "natural gas," companies exploit the word’s positive connotations to create a false perception of cleanliness and sustainability. This linguistic manipulation effectively distances the product from its identity as a potent fossil fuel, allowing the industry to promote methane as a viable "transition" energy source despite scientific consensus on the urgent need to eliminate all fossil fuel consumption. This disinformation campaign has proven remarkably effective in shaping public opinion, with studies indicating that people view the fuel favorably when labeled "natural gas" but associate the term "methane" with climate harm. The industry has consistently funded marketing efforts to reinforce this narrative, capitalizing on the fuel’s invisibility in daily life and its historical reputation as a cleaner alternative to coal. As a result, the public remains largely ignorant of the extraction methods, such as fracking, and the significant methane leaks that contribute heavily to global warming, illustrating how language is used to obscure critical ecological realities. The relevance to greenwashing lies in the deliberate use of ambiguous language to deceive consumers and policymakers about a product’s true environmental impact. The author advocates for banning the term "natural gas" in favor of "fossil gas" to strip away the misleading "natural" association and force transparency. This perspective highlights how greenwashing is not just about false claims of efficiency, but also about framing the very identity of the fuel to maintain public acceptance and hinder regulatory action, ultimately delaying the necessary transition away from carbon-intensive energy sources.

Lack of understanding of new EU anti-greenwashing rules
The Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD) fundamentally reshapes corporate transparency by mandating detailed disclosures on environmental and social impacts, directly targeting greenwashing. By requiring extensive, standardized reporting, the regulation aims to eliminate misleading sustainability claims, ensuring that companies provide verifiable evidence of their actual contributions to human rights and ecological standards. This shift moves beyond voluntary disclosures to enforceable accountability, making it significantly harder for businesses to hide unsustainable practices behind vague marketing. However, significant implementation hurdles remain, as many firms underestimate the complexity and resource intensity of compliance. A large portion of surveyed companies lack sufficient data infrastructure and understanding of the new rigorous audit requirements, with independent third-party verification becoming mandatory. This gap between regulatory expectations and corporate capability highlights a critical vulnerability: without adequate preparation, organizations risk failing to meet the stringent proof standards intended to curb deceptive advertising. The relevance to greenwashing lies in the introduction of mandatory external assurance, which serves as a powerful deterrent against fabrication. By forcing companies to undergo strict auditing and face severe penalties for non-compliance, the law attacks the root of greenwashing: unchecked, unverified claims. Consequently, businesses must prioritize accurate data collection and transparent reporting to avoid reputational damage, ensuring that sustainability assertions are substantiated by hard evidence rather than superficial narrative.

The next wave of greenwashing: offsets, competitor claims and 'transition-washing'
New guidelines for Asian financiers expose emerging greenwashing tactics that threaten market integrity and trust. Key issues include "transition-washing," where capital for carbon-intensive firms fails to drive genuine decarbonization, and "greenwashing by association," where asset managers legitimize unsustainable practices through selective portfolio inclusion. These malpractices divert resources from effective climate action, creating long-term financial risks and undermining the credibility of the growing green economy. The article highlights that regulatory frameworks alone are insufficient due to the lack of clear definitions for terms like "green" and "sustainability." Without standardized criteria, companies can make misleading claims, and regulators struggle to enforce compliance in a fragmented Asian market. This ambiguity allows firms to exploit loopholes, such as using excessive carbon offsets without reducing emissions, making it difficult for investors to distinguish between genuine sustainability efforts and superficial marketing strategies. Relevance to greenwashing lies in the urgent need for stricter due diligence and harmonized global standards. Financial institutions must look beyond self-reported disclosures and critically evaluate whether corporate actions align with climate goals. As NGOs and regulators begin to penalize misleading claims, the focus shifts toward accountability, emphasizing that transparency and consistent internal actions are essential to preventing the erosion of public trust and ensuring that capital truly supports the energy transition.
