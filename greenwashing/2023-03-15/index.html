Funds set to walk back ethical claims amid ‘greenwashing’ crackdown
Super funds and investment firms are increasingly withdrawing or toning down their ethical investing claims due to a strict regulatory crackdown on greenwashing. Fearful of facing fines or legal action from regulators like ASIC, many managers are adopting a more cautious approach to their marketing language. This shift is driven by the growing risk that broad sustainability promises may not withstand legal scrutiny, prompting firms to avoid potential "backing into a corner" scenarios where they cannot meet prescriptive international commitments. This phenomenon, termed "greenhushing," highlights the tension between market demand for ethical products and the stringent legal requirements for transparency. While some analysts argue this retreat from bold claims is healthy because it forces more precise and accurate terminology, critics warn that firms should not use regulatory pressure as an excuse to step away from genuine sustainability commitments. The core issue remains the difficulty for consumers to distinguish between funds that merely use safer language and those that truly deliver on environmental goals, creating confusion in the marketplace. The article is highly relevant to greenwashing as it illustrates the evolving landscape of how misleading claims are being exposed and penalized. It demonstrates that greenwashing is no longer just a reputational risk but a serious legal liability, forcing the industry to either significantly improve its transparency and operational alignment with its marketing or reduce its public assertions. This regulatory tightening aims to restore trust by ensuring that sustainable investing labels reflect actual practices rather than mere marketing strategies, thereby holding financial institutions accountable for their environmental impact claims.

Super funds failing to engage with fossil fuel companies on net zero

This lack of effective engagement creates legal vulnerabilities, exposing institutions to potential lawsuits for failing to align investments with stated environmental commitments. The findings highlight a critical disconnect between corporate promises and actual investment influence, illustrating how passive oversight facilitates deceptive environmental branding.

Without recyclers, EPR regulations will simply facilitate greenwashing - IT-Online
South Africa’s Extended Producer Responsibility Regulations, while well-intentioned, risk facilitating greenwashing if implemented without a robust recycling infrastructure. Simply holding producers legally accountable for product lifecycles is insufficient if the downstream systems required to actually recycle that waste do not exist. This regulatory gap allows companies to appear compliant on paper while failing to deliver tangible environmental benefits, thereby undermining the genuine goals of the circular economy. The core danger lies in the disconnect between policy and practice. Without skilled service providers and adequate processing capacity, materials collected by consumers may still end up in landfills, rendering producer commitments meaningless. This scenario creates a false impression of environmental stewardship, where brands can claim adherence to sustainability standards despite lacking the logistical support to ensure proper material recovery. Consequently, regulations alone cannot prevent misleading environmental claims if the physical mechanisms for execution are missing. True progress requires deep collaboration between producers, public entities, and private service providers, including the informal waste sector. Public-private partnerships can enhance collection efficiency, attract necessary investment, and integrate existing informal networks into formal systems. This holistic approach ensures that recycling capacities expand effectively, turning regulatory compliance into real-world impact. By focusing on practical execution rather than just legal adherence, stakeholders can prevent greenwashing and foster genuine economic and environmental growth.
