The article examines the competitive landscape between liquid hydrogen and ammonia as critical vectors for the US energy transition toward net-zero emissions. Driven by recent federal incentives, both fuels are positioned to replace hydrocarbons in sectors like maritime transport, power generation, and aerospace. However, their success hinges on overcoming significant differences in storage requirements, existing infrastructure, and immediate safety protocols, with ammonia currently offering logistical advantages due to established agricultural distribution networks. Despite hydrogen’s theoretical superiority as an energy carrier, its technical challenges regarding cryogenic storage and leakage make ammonia an attractive alternative for transportation and storage. The analysis highlights that hydrogen infrastructure is less developed, often requiring costly retrofits of existing natural gas facilities. Consequently, ammonia’s lower handling requirements and stable industrial demand provide a more immediate solution for decarbonizing heavy industries, suggesting a symbiotic rather than purely competitive relationship where ammonia may serve as a hydrogen carrier during the transition phase. This dynamic is highly relevant to greenwashing discussions because it reveals the risk of "if you build it, they will come" market strategies accelerating before true demand exists. The article notes a recent surge in production capacity outpacing immediate agricultural needs, causing price volatility and investment uncertainty. This highlights how corporate and governmental pushes for clean energy solutions can create speculative bubbles, where the environmental benefits are assumed rather than validated by sustainable market equilibrium, potentially leading to wasted resources or misleading claims about the viability and readiness of these specific clean energy commodities.
