News about greenwashing on 2023-07-21


The Australian Competition and Consumer Commission (ACCC) has issued new guidance targeting the prevalence of misleading environmental claims, following reports that over half of reviewed businesses made concerning assertions about their sustainability practices. This regulatory move underscores a critical shift in how authorities view greenwashing, establishing that vague or unqualified statements are increasingly likely to trigger enforcement actions under the Australian Consumer Law. Central to this framework is the mandate for absolute accuracy and substantiation, requiring businesses to back every claim with robust, scientific evidence rather than relying on broad labels like "green" or "eco-friendly." The ACCC emphasizes that companies must consider the entire lifecycle of a product, ensuring that partial benefits do not mask overall environmental harm, and warns against using small print to contradict prominent marketing messages. This guidance is highly relevant to greenwashing because it transforms general ethical concerns into specific legal liabilities with severe financial penalties. By clarifying that aspirational goals or limited benefits cannot justify sweeping claims, the ACCC is actively curbing deceptive marketing practices, forcing organizations to align their public messaging with their actual operational realities to avoid significant regulatory consequences.

ACCC publishes draft guidance to improve businesses' environmental claims - Beef Central
The ACCC has released draft guidance to combat greenwashing, addressing a recent sweep that revealed over half of surveyed businesses were making potentially misleading environmental claims. This initiative aims to protect consumer trust and ensure market fairness, as deceptive marketing allows dishonest firms to charge premium prices while unfairly disadvantaging companies investing in genuine sustainable practices. Central to the new framework are eight practical principles encouraging transparency and evidence-based marketing. Businesses are urged to ensure all claims are accurate, clear, and substantiable, aligning their operations with Australian Consumer Law obligations. The guidance emphasizes that if a company cannot verify an environmental benefit, it should refrain from making the claim entirely to avoid misleading the public. This update is crucial for understanding greenwashing because it shifts the regulatory focus from merely identifying violations to providing clear, preventive standards for compliance. By soliciting feedback from both businesses and consumers, the ACCC aims to foster an environment where legitimate sustainability efforts are distinguished from exaggerated marketing, thereby reducing ambiguity and enhancing accountability in the marketplace.

Government’s Climate Active program should be probed for potential greenwashing, Allan Fels says
The article highlights serious concerns that Australia’s government-backed Climate Active carbon neutral certification program facilitates greenwashing. Critics, including former Australian Competition and Consumer Commission chair Allan Fels, argue that the scheme allows companies to claim carbon neutrality primarily through purchasing offsets rather than achieving genuine emissions reductions. This approach effectively permits high-emission industries, including fossil fuel giants, to label themselves as environmentally responsible without implementing substantive decarbonization strategies. The core issue lies in the program’s failure to enforce absolute emission cuts, which contradicts scientific imperatives for immediate action against climate change. By prioritizing offsetting over reduction, the certification creates a misleading impression of corporate environmental stewardship. This discrepancy is further exacerbated by the removal of promotional language describing certified entities as "climate champions," suggesting an awareness that the current labeling may not withstand scrutiny under consumer protection laws regarding misleading conduct. This situation is critical to understanding greenwashing because it demonstrates how institutional endorsements can legitimize superficial environmental claims. When a government authority certifies products as "carbon neutral" without demanding rigorous internal reductions, it risks deceiving consumers and diluting the integrity of climate action. The call for an independent investigation underscores the urgent need for transparent standards that prioritize actual performance over purchased credits, ensuring that environmental marketing reflects genuine sustainability efforts rather than strategic reputational management.

NGOs Call for Stronger Standards on Net-Zero Targets, Urging SBTi to Align with UN Recommendations
Non-governmental organizations are urging the Science Based Targets initiative to align its methodologies with United Nations recommendations to prevent greenwashing. The UN High Level Expert Group advocates for stricter standards, requiring companies to report 100% of supply chain emissions and set specific targets for all significant greenhouse gases, including methane. However, NGOs argue that the SBTi currently permits companies to ignore methane and report only a fraction of their supply chain emissions, directly contradicting the rigorous scientific consensus needed to address the climate emergency. The discrepancy between the SBTi’s flexible approach and the UN’s strict proposals creates loopholes that allow major polluters to mislead the public. Critics highlight that the initiative continues to validate targets aligned with warmer scenarios, despite promoting a baseline consistent with limiting global warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius. This misalignment enables corporations to present credible-seeming climate commitments that are effectively weaker than necessary, thereby diluting the urgency of global climate action and risking the feasibility of international temperature goals. Consequently, the SBTi faces accusations of facilitating greenwashing, particularly when companies use its validation to claim progress without delivering substantive results. Instances where corporations continue to assert net-zero pledges long after submission deadlines have passed, despite lacking verified plans or facing legal challenges, illustrate the scheme’s vulnerabilities. For consumers and investors, this article is vital as it reveals how established verification mechanisms can be exploited, urging a demand for transparent, scientifically robust standards to ensure genuine corporate accountability rather than superficial environmental branding.

Venezuela: el “greenwashing” de Maduro – DW – 20/07/2023
The article argues that Venezuela’s recent military operations to combat illegal mining in the Amazon are not genuine conservation efforts but a calculated campaign of greenwashing. By projecting an image of environmental protection, the Maduro regime aims to access international climate funds and legitimize its image ahead of the Amazon Summit, despite the continued expansion of destructive mining activities under military supervision. The core implication is that these actions serve primarily as a political maneuver to control mineral resources and generate revenue, rather than to restore ecosystems. Environmental organizations highlight that the state lacks a real strategy to stop the ecological damage, indicating that the government is prioritizing economic survival through extractive industries over the irreversible destruction caused by illegal mining, violence, and mercury pollution. This situation is critically relevant to the study of greenwashing as it illustrates how governments use symbolic environmental policies to mask harmful practices. The article demonstrates that when environmental rhetoric decouples from actual policy implementation—such as maintaining mining decrees and rejecting international climate agreements—the resulting "green" initiatives are merely PR tools designed to secure financial resources without addressing the underlying environmental crisis.
