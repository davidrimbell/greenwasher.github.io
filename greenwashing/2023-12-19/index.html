'Consumers should be discerning enough': Prism+ defends air-con ad after watchdog deemed it 'greenwashing'
The Advertising Standards Authority of Singapore has officially deemed Prism+’s promotional campaign for its air-conditioners as greenwashing, marking the country’s first regulatory breach in this category. The authority ruled that claiming the product was the “best tip” to save the earth exaggerated its environmental impact, misleading consumers by implying a direct, significant ecological benefit from mere usage habits. This decision underscores that vague or exaggerated sustainability claims in advertising are unacceptable, regardless of whether the product holds high energy-efficiency ratings. Prism+ defended the advertisement as satirical humor, arguing that consumers should recognize that no single appliance can significantly save the planet. However, regulators maintained that such messaging creates ambiguity and potential misinformation, emphasizing that environmental claims must be substantiated by independent testing within local contexts. The case highlights the tension between marketing narratives and factual accuracy, establishing a precedent that even playful or ironic content cannot bypass the prohibition on misleading environmental representations. This ruling is highly relevant to greenwashing discourse as it demonstrates increasing regulatory scrutiny and the enforcement of advertising codes against dubious sustainability marketing. By taking action in a high-visibility case involving a popular influencer, the authority signals that companies can no longer rely on ambiguity or humor to obscure unsubstantiated eco-claims. This mirrors a global trend where regulators are actively clamping down on exaggerated environmental commitments, urging brands to ensure their green marketing is transparent, accurate, and scientifically backed.

This enforcement highlights the growing regulatory scrutiny faced by the aviation sector as its carbon footprint remains significant despite post-pandemic recovery efforts. This case is crucial to understanding greenwashing because it illustrates how companies often use ambiguous language to create a false impression of ecological responsibility. The authorities ruled that such tactics violate advertising codes by hiding the lack of substantial environmental action behind marketing hype. Consequently, businesses must ensure their sustainability claims are backed by verifiable data rather than relying on vague promises that may deceive consumers. Furthermore, this precedent signals increased legal risks for other travel industry stakeholders, including distributors and intermediaries. Even entities merely relaying airline or hotel claims may face accountability if the underlying assertions are deemed false. This expansion of liability warns the broader tourism sector that facilitating or amplifying unverified green marketing can lead to serious legal consequences, forcing a reevaluation of how environmental messaging is constructed and distributed across the industry.
