Does oil-rich Saudi Arabia really want to mend its ways and go green?

Does oil-rich Saudi Arabia really want to mend its ways and go green?

The article examines The Line, a flagship project in Saudi Arabia’s Neom initiative, which promises a zero-carbon, car-free urban environment. While the vision of 100% renewable energy and sustainable living appears impressive, the physical design raises significant ecological and practical concerns. The linear structure threatens to fragment wildlife habitats and create inefficient urban living conditions, suggesting that the environmental benefits may be more theoretical than tangible. Despite these structural flaws, the project is heavily criticized as greenwashing, masking traditional economic reliance and authoritarian governance with futuristic sustainability branding. The narrative highlights the tension between Saudi Arabia’s genuine efforts toward energy transition and its enduring social restrictions. By juxtaposing progressive energy goals with the repression of basic civil liberties, the text illustrates how large-scale environmental projects can serve as distractions from broader human rights issues. This case is relevant to greenwashing because it exemplifies how nations may leverage eco-friendly rhetoric to rebrand their global image without addressing fundamental societal problems. The disconnect between the projected environmental ideal and the reality of the landscape design underscores the risk of accepting ambitious sustainability claims at face value. It serves as a cautionary tale about the potential for grand infrastructure projects to prioritize marketing over meaningful ecological or social progress.
