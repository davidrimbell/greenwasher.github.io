In a first, California counts on carbon capture to meet its climate goals

California’s ambitious plan to achieve carbon neutrality by 2045 relies heavily on carbon capture and storage (CCS), a strategy experts widely criticize as risky and premature. The state government acknowledges that CCS deployment is uncertain due to significant financial, regulatory, and technical hurdles. However, regulators continue to count on this unproven technology to bridge the gap for emissions they cannot otherwise eliminate, particularly from persistent fossil fuel infrastructure. This approach draws sharp criticism for gambling on a technology that is not yet commercially viable at scale, effectively treating speculative future capabilities as current solutions. The discrepancy between California’s aggressive modeling and reality highlights a core issue in greenwashing: presenting theoretical capabilities as implemented facts. While the state assumes high capture rates and rapid infrastructure deployment, no commercial-scale CCS plants currently operate in California or globally. Pilot projects consistently fall short of the efficiency levels required by policy, capturing significantly less carbon than projected. By relying on optimistic assumptions about feasibility and speed, the plan risks overstating the state’s progress and potential, creating a false sense of security regarding its ability to meet strict interim and long-term climate targets. This controversy illustrates how greenwashing can emerge when policymakers prioritize political narratives over technological realities. The reliance on CCS allows for the continued justification of fossil fuel use under the guise of mitigation, shifting the burden of climate action onto unproven industrial fixes rather than demanding immediate reductions. Furthermore, the high costs and energy penalties associated with CCS, combined with limited federal subsidies, suggest that these projects may ultimately burden ratepayers without delivering promised environmental benefits. Consequently, the state’s strategy risks appearing more environmentally responsible than it is, masking the limitations of its decarbonization roadmap.
