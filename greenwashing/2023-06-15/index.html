Beyond Greenwashing: Exposing Deceptive Climate Claims and the Path to Accountability

Sustainable finance is a tricky topic. “Greenwashing” creates risks and chances for buyers. This happens when people are looking for products. Big energy companies like Shell show the bad side of this. Shell plans to make more natural gas. It also wants to keep oil production steady. This goes against its promise to reach net-zero by 2050. This move tricks people about the climate crisis. The company relies on unproven tech instead of acting now. This lies hurt consumer trust. It also slows down real efforts to cut emissions.

Look at the lawsuit against Delta Air Lines. The suit says the airline’s marketing is deceptive. Delta uses carbon offsets to claim it is carbon-neutral. But these offsets lack “additionality.” This means the funded projects would have happened anyway. This is a transactional lie. It stops real cleaning of the air. It hurts customers who pay more for flights. These flights are only “green” on paper.

Some experts see an upside to greenwashing. They say it helps change how people feel. Filling media with green messages shifts minds. It moves people from pure buying to caring for the planet. This is true for younger generations. Even if the corporate plans are weak, the messaging might work. It could push people to change their habits. This view asks us to look past the lies. Can this “emotional rewiring” lead to real action?

However, deceptive marketing scares the market. Fewer new sustainable funds are launching. Issuers fear being accused of greenwashing. This fear leads to fund reclassifications. Companies want to avoid bad reputations. This leaves investors with fewer true green options. This is ironic. Skepticism is needed, but it stops new products from developing.

Consumers must demand better proof. Third-party risk management (TPRM) helps. It ensures supply chains are transparent. Experts say businesses need strong frameworks. These should verify ethical standards. Claims must be credible. Consumers should support companies that train employees. They should also support those using tech to check data. Avoid companies with vague promises.

Governments also play a big role. In the Philippines, the Maharlika Investment Fund bill is a good example. It uses future development talk to hide opaque finances. Critics say this fund bets on risky investments. It does not spend on social needs directly. It risks stability because there are no national savings. The fund is framed as key for attracting money. This hides immediate social costs. It replaces real welfare with high-risk experiments.

Fossil fuel companies use image laundering. We need strict laws to protect buyers. Experts warn that voluntary promises are not enough. Self-regulation fails. Corporate interests can sabotage climate efforts. The UK and EU are trying to ban misleading ads. They want to regulate the whole financing chain. This is a model for stricter rules. Buyers in the consideration stage can help. Look for proof. Hold corporations and governments accountable.

The world we leave for our children matters. It should not be built on illusions. It must be built on honesty. We need genuine care for the planet. We must see through the greenwash. Demand a future that is clean, sustainable, and ethical.
