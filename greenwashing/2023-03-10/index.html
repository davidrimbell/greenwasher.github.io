The European Consumer Organisation argues that labeling food products as CO2 neutral constitutes greenwashing. This practice relies on carbon offsetting, where companies pay for future environmental projects to balance current emissions. Such claims are scientifically inaccurate and misleading, as consumers cannot verify the actual environmental impact or validity of these offsets. Consumers often trust these labels, assuming they indicate genuine ecological benefits. However, the underlying logic is flawed because offsetting allows immediate pollution in exchange for uncertain future compensation. This marketing strategy exploits consumer concern for the environment without delivering substantive climate action, creating a false sense of responsibility. Consequently, BEUC calls for a complete ban on these misleading labels. The relevance to greenwashing lies in exposing how corporations use vague environmental claims to mask unsustainable practices. By highlighting the gap between marketing promises and actual carbon reduction, the report warns against deceptive sustainability tactics that hinder genuine ecological progress.

Simultaneously, a Stuttgart court ruled that Commerz Real failed to adequately explain the methodology behind its klimaVest fund’s environmental impact calculations. Corporate greenwashing—misusing ‘net zero’ pledges
A recent analysis reveals that major global corporations are largely engaging in greenwashing through misleading “net zero” and carbon-neutral claims. Rather than committing to the deep decarbonization required to meet Paris Agreement goals, these companies rely heavily on questionable offsetting and insetting mechanisms. These practices allow firms to claim neutrality while reducing their actual emissions by only a fraction, effectively continuing their significant climate impact under the guise of environmental responsibility. The core of this deception lies in the reliance on carbon credits, which often lack permanence and suffer from flawed science and imprecise accounting. For instance, offsets based on forest conservation are vulnerable to wildfires, rendering the claimed neutrality invalid when carbon is re-released. Furthermore, many “carbon-neutral” labels apply to a negligible percentage of a company’s total footprint, creating a false sense of completeness. This misleading marketing confuses consumers, who may feel justified in maintaining harmful consumption habits because they believe they are supporting sustainable practices. This article is critically relevant to greenwashing as it exposes how ambiguous terminology obscures the truth and shifts blame onto consumers. The report argues for strict regulatory interventions, particularly by the European Union, to ban vague neutrality claims and prohibit the use of offsets in future performance marketing. By enforcing transparent communication that distinguishes between genuine emission cuts and financial donations to projects, policymakers can prevent companies from using greenwashing to avoid substantive climate action and protect the integrity of the green transition.

These companies claim to be 'carbon neutral'. But the devil is in the details
Major Australian corporations leverage government-backed Climate Active certification to claim carbon neutrality, a practice critics argue constitutes greenwashing. The core issue lies in the narrow "emissions boundaries" of the certification, which primarily cover direct operational emissions while excluding significant Scope 3 emissions, such those from fuel combustion or investment portfolios. This selective accounting allows polluting industries to present a misleadingly clean image, ignoring the vast majority of their climate impact. This discrepancy creates a deceptive narrative for consumers who reasonably expect "carbon neutral" to encompass all business activities. Legal experts suggest this gap between technical certification and public perception may violate consumer protection laws, as ordinary people interpret these labels as holistic environmental commitments rather than narrow operational metrics. Consequently, companies can market themselves as sustainable leaders while continuing fossil fuel expansion and high-carbon operations, effectively obscuring their true environmental footprint. Furthermore, the certification relies heavily on low-integrity carbon offsets rather than actual emission reductions, violating the mitigation hierarchy intended to prioritize direct cuts. With evidence showing that some offset projects fail to deliver promised sequestration, the system enables businesses to bypass structural changes needed for genuine sustainability. This reliance on questionable offsets extends the lifespan of polluting industries, undermining the climate goals the certification purports to support.
