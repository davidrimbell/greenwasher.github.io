A car-free town in the Amazon serves lessons for pedaling to net zero emissions

The article challenges the conventional association between modernization and environmental sustainability by highlighting Afuá, a Brazilian city that rejects motor vehicles to maintain its ecological balance. Rather than viewing a car-free status as a sign of underdevelopment, the narrative positions Afuá as a pragmatic model for low-carbon living, demonstrating that preserving traditional water-based transport infrastructure can effectively minimize urban emissions and traffic fatalities. This case study exposes the dangers of the "tarmac temptation," where road construction is often mistaken for essential progress despite driving deforestation and climate vulnerability. By refusing heavy machinery and infrastructure that facilitate fossil-fuel dependency, Afuá offers a tangible alternative to the destructive urban sprawl seen elsewhere in the Amazon, proving that community-led decisions can successfully decouple local development from carbon-intensive growth models. Relevance to greenwashing lies in reframing what constitutes genuine sustainability versus superficial environmental branding. While many entities claim green credentials while expanding carbon-heavy infrastructure, Afuá’s authentic, structural rejection of combustion engines serves as a stark contrast to empty eco-marketing. It underscores that true climate action often requires resisting political and economic pressures for visible, fossil-fueled "progress" in favor of holistic, community-consensus solutions that address both environmental and social realities.
