The push to standardize ESG scores could make corporate greenwashing easier, not harder

The widespread adoption of ESG-linked executive compensation aims to align leadership incentives with sustainability goals, but this practice introduces significant risks of strategic manipulation. When performance metrics are standardized and public, executives can easily "game" the system by optimizing for specific indicators rather than achieving genuine environmental or social improvement. This creates a distortion where resources are diverted toward appearances of compliance rather than actual impact, exacerbating the agency problem between shareholders and management. This dynamic reveals a counterintuitive benefit of the current fragmented landscape of ESG rating agencies. Because different providers use distinct methodologies, it becomes significantly harder for executives to simultaneously manipulate multiple scores without making substantive changes to their operations. The lack of consensus among raters serves as a disciplinary mechanism, forcing leaders to address a broader range of issues rather than focusing narrowly on a single, predictable metric. The article is relevant to greenwashing because it challenges the regulatory push for harmonization, arguing that it may inadvertently facilitate superficial compliance. By consolidating standards, regulators risk creating a single, easily gamed target that allows corporations to maintain the image of sustainability while avoiding meaningful action. Consequently, preserving independent, competing metrics may be more effective in preventing greenwashing than imposing a unified, standardized framework that executives can systematically exploit.
