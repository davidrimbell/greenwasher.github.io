Canada Amends the Competition Act to Target Greenwashing

Canada has significantly tightened regulations around environmental marketing by amending the Competition Act to explicitly require companies to substantiate green claims. This legal shift establishes new standards for assessing environmental benefits but notably leaves key criteria, such as "adequate and proper substantiation," undefined. Consequently, corporations face heightened uncertainty regarding compliance, as the burden of proof rests on them to demonstrate that their claims are not misleading. The introduction of private litigation powers allows individuals and organizations to challenge greenwashing practices directly, subjecting violators to severe financial penalties and reputational damage. While this mechanism aims to increase corporate accountability, it has sparked fears of "greenhushing," where businesses may silently retreat from communicating environmental goals to avoid the risk of legal action. Companies are therefore compelled to meticulously review and potentially downplay their sustainability communications across all platforms. This article is crucial for understanding the evolving landscape of greenwashing because it transforms vague marketing assertions into legally actionable liabilities. By enabling third-party enforcement and imposing heavy fines, the law creates a powerful deterrent against deceptive environmental practices. However, the lack of clear definitions introduces complexity, forcing organizations to navigate a precarious balance between transparent sustainability reporting and the potential risks of litigation.
