Greenwashing
EN ES

The COP29 climate summit in Azerbaijan concluded not with a breakthrough, but with a significant diplomatic failure, highlighting deep fractures between wealthy nations and developing countries over climate finance. The central point of contention was the "loss and damage" fund, intended to compensate vulnerable nations for climate-induced destruction. The offer of $300 billion was widely rejected by developing nations as insufficient, revealing a massive gap between the financial commitments made by industrialized states and the actual costs of climate adaptation faced by those least responsible for emissions. This impasse underscores the broader issue of accountability in international climate policy, particularly regarding who should pay for historical emissions. Disagreements persisted over the definition of "developing" versus "developed" nations, with major emitters like China retaining status that exempts them from binding financial obligations. Furthermore, the lack of clarity on whether funds would be grants or loans, combined with disputes over private sector involvement, demonstrates how vague commitments allow powerful economies to avoid substantive responsibility while maintaining the appearance of progress. The relevance to greenwashing is stark: the summit’s outcome serves as a prime example of performative environmentalism. By allowing a fossil-fuel-rich host nation to dictate terms and avoiding specific language on phase-outs, the negotiations prioritized political expediency over genuine decarbonization. This suggests that global climate conferences are increasingly being used to legitimize continued reliance on dirty energy sources, masking inaction behind complex, unenforced financial frameworks that fail to address the urgency of the crisis.
