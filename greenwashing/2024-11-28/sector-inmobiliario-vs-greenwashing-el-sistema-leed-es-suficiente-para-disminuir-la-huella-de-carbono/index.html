Sector inmobiliario vs greenwashing: ¿El sistema LEED es suficiente para disminuir la huella de carbono?

The article argues that widely used sustainability certifications like LEED are insufficient to mitigate the substantial carbon footprint of the construction sector, which accounts for a significant portion of global energy-related emissions. Experts highlight that this gap has fueled greenwashing, as developers often market properties as "green" to appeal to conscious consumers without implementing the deep structural changes required for genuine environmental responsibility. The core issue is that current standards allow buildings to appear sustainable while continuing to pollute, misleading buyers who lack the information to distinguish between marketing rhetoric and actual ecological impact. To address these limitations, the text introduces the Living Community Challenge as a superior, regenerative alternative to traditional certifications. This rigorous model demands that developments not only reduce harm but actively contribute more resources than they consume, pushing beyond mere efficiency toward true ecological restoration. The article cites a specific project in Mexico that has achieved this distinction, demonstrating how such frameworks can enforce stricter accountability and prevent the superficial application of sustainability labels that characterize much of the industry’s current practices. This case study is relevant to greenwashing because it illustrates the difference between compliance with basic standards and genuine regenerative design. By showcasing a project that achieves full energy and water autonomy alongside significant efficiency gains, the article exposes how lower-tier certifications can mask ongoing environmental damage. It underscores the urgent need for consumers and regulators to scrutinize the depth of sustainability claims, revealing that without higher benchmarks, "green" labels often serve as tools for deception rather than indicators of meaningful climate action.
