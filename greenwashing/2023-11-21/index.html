New investigation casts doubt on a Singapore-listed palm oil giant’s green claims - ICIJ
This investigation exposes how First Resources, a Singapore-listed palm oil producer claiming sustainable practices, secretly controls Indonesian firms responsible for widespread rainforest destruction. Despite public pledges to protect the environment, the company’s majority shareholders utilize complex offshore structures to hide their ownership of deforesters, creating a misleading narrative of transparency while actively contributing to ecological harm and indigenous rights violations. The scandal highlights a critical regulatory loophole allowing listed companies to publish unaudited sustainability reports, rendering green claims unreliable for investors and consumers. This lack of independent verification enables corporations to certify operations linked to illegal logging and human rights abuses, demonstrating how the current sustainability industry often prioritizes marketing veneers over actual environmental compliance through flawed auditing mechanisms. This case is relevant to greenwashing because it illustrates how powerful entities exploit weak reporting standards and corporate secrecy to mislead stakeholders. By obscuring their involvement in deforestation through shell companies and relying on voluntary, unverified certifications, businesses like First Resources perpetuate a system where profit-driven environmental degradation is concealed behind superficial commitments to sustainability, deceiving both the market and the public.

Sustainability in spirits: unpacking the buzzwords - The Spirits Business
The spirits industry is experiencing a surge in sustainability marketing, yet significant challenges remain in distinguishing genuine environmental progress from greenwashing. Major global brands often score poorly in independent ethical rankings, suggesting that much of their publicized effort lacks substantive impact. This discrepancy highlights a critical need for transparency, as consumers struggle to verify claims amidst complex supply chains and opaque reporting practices. A central component of this deception is the overreliance on carbon offsetting, which experts warn serves as a convenient substitute for actual decarbonization. Rather than reducing emissions at the source through operational changes, companies frequently use offsets to claim carbon neutrality, a practice critics label as misleading. True sustainability requires prioritizing tangible reductions in Scope 3 emissions and avoiding the use of credits as a primary strategy, which allows brands to bypass the difficult work of redesigning their processes and supply chains. Relevant to greenwashing, this article exposes the gap between aspirational marketing and measurable action. It argues that without rigorous measurement and a focus on immediate, low-hanging fruit initiatives, sustainability claims risk becoming false advertising. The piece advocates for a shift toward long-term infrastructure investments and transparent accounting, warning that companies relying on vague promises or superficial offsets will eventually face accountability and reputational damage as consumer scrutiny intensifies.

The False Promise of Carbon Offsets
The article argues that temporary carbon offsets, which include forestry and renewable energy projects, fail as genuine climate solutions because they only delay emissions rather than eliminating them. These mechanisms rely on unverifiable baseline estimates, making them susceptible to manipulation and gross overestimation of emission reductions. Consequently, they offer a false sense of progress while actual atmospheric greenhouse gas concentrations continue to rise, ultimately undermining global decarbonization efforts. This failure is particularly relevant to greenwashing, as these temporary offsets provide political and corporate cover for major emitters. By purchasing credits for short-term projects, companies can claim environmental responsibility and "net zero" status without undergoing the difficult transition away from fossil fuels. This allows bad actors to maintain high emission levels while appearing sustainable, effectively shifting the burden of climate action to developing nations and delaying necessary systemic changes. To combat this, the author urges governments to phase out all non-permanent offsets and strictly regulate the voluntary carbon market. Instead of relying on flawed credits, funds should be redirected toward permanent carbon removal technologies and international climate aid. Ending the use of temporary offsets will remove the primary tool for greenwashing, forcing corporations to prioritize actual emission reductions and invest in lasting solutions rather than accounting tricks.
