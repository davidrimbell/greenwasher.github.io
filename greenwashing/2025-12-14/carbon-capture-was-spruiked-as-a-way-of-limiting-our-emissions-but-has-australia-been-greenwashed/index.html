Carbon capture was spruiked as a way of limiting our emissions – but has Australia been greenwashed?

The Gorgon carbon capture and storage project, despite billions in investment and government support, has failed to meet its emission reduction targets, with storage volumes declining significantly since inception. This underperformance highlights a critical discrepancy between the technological promises marketed by energy giants like Chevron and the actual, deteriorating results on the ground. The project serves as a prominent case study demonstrating that even advanced, high-profile industrial initiatives can struggle with technical viability and operational consistency. Critics and independent analysts argue that carbon capture is increasingly being used as greenwashing to justify the continued expansion of fossil fuel infrastructure rather than serving as a legitimate climate solution. With the technology capturing only a tiny fraction of total emissions and often aiding enhanced oil recovery, it functions more as a tool for extending the life of polluting industries than as a meaningful mechanism for decarbonization. This reality undermines political and corporate narratives that position carbon capture as a primary pathway to net-zero goals. This case is highly relevant to greenwashing because it reveals how substantial public and private funding is directed toward technologies that offer negligible climate benefits. By labeling inefficient and ineffective projects as essential solutions, the fossil fuel industry maintains public support and political lobbying power while delaying the transition to cheaper, genuine renewable energy alternatives. Ultimately, the Gorgon failure illustrates the risk of relying on unproven, large-scale industrial fixes that appear proactive but remain climatically irrelevant.
