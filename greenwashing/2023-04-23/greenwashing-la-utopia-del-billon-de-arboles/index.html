¿Greenwashing? La utopía del billón de árboles

The initiative driven by business leaders and governments to plant one trillion trees has become a paradigmatic example of greenwashing, using reforestation as a marketing strategy to promote purported corporate sustainability without necessarily reducing actual emissions. Many companies leverage these promises to offset their carbon footprints and achieve “net zero,” creating an illusion of environmental responsibility while continuing their polluting practices. This shift in focus suggests that the primary objective is public image rather than ecological effectiveness. Experts warn that these campaigns clash with severe physical and biological limitations, such as the scarcity of available land and the low survival rate of planted trees. The emphasis on active “restoration” rather than the protection of existing forests, combined with the creation of monocultures, threatens biodiversity and often disregards the needs of local communities. The reality is that trees take decades to absorb carbon significantly, and many die or are cut down before delivering the promised climate impact. The relevance of this article lies in debunking the myth that mass tree planting is a quick and effective solution to the climate crisis. By exposing how ecological and social complexities are erroneously simplified in public relations campaigns, it highlights the need to prioritize direct emission reductions and the conservation of mature ecosystems. This critique is essential for identifying and combating greenwashing strategies that distract from genuine climate action.
