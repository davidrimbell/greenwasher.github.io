Beyond the Label: How Vague Claims and Small Gestures Distort the Market for Eco-Friendly Products

People who want to buy eco-friendly products often face a confusing market. Many companies make environmental claims that are hard to check. This confusion helps and hurts shoppers at different steps in their buying process.

Big companies use vague words to attract shoppers who care about nature. For example, some producers sell “blue hydrogen.” This energy creates a lot of pollution. But they call it “low-carbon” because there are no clear rules for clean energy. This trick helps these companies sell more products. Shoppers benefit because this attention pushes companies to compete and improve their image. However, this is risky. Without clear definitions, it is hard to tell if a product is truly new or just a marketing trick. The European Union tries to fix this by requiring proof. But without strong tracking technology, it is still difficult to verify these claims. When buyers trust unclear labels, they might accidentally support fossil fuels instead of real sustainability.

Some companies use small gestures to look good while hiding bigger problems. McDonald’s works with a group called Initiative Tierwohl. This looks like they care about ethical farming. But it hides other environmental issues in their business. In Malta, “Project Green” cleans up valleys. This looks nice, but the government still allows harmful development on public land. These actions create a false sense of progress. Shoppers feel safe knowing their money supports “ethical” practices. But this feeling is wrong. These small steps distract from real problems like habitat loss. Buyers who accept these surface changes do not fix the bad business models behind them.

Money tricks also make things complicated. Verizon wants to build a wind farm, even though they do not need the power. They buy cheap electricity to look green. This does not reduce their carbon footprint. Instead, it shakes up local energy markets. For consumers, this brings some good news. It makes renewable energy more visible. It also pressures other companies to go green to stay in business. But there is a bad side. It distorts the market. Buyers might support companies that care more about image than impact. This wastes resources and confuses people who want to spend money on things that truly help the planet.

The tech industry acts the same way. Artificial intelligence needs a lot of power. But tech giants rush to market with promises of innovation. Companies like Oracle and DeepSeek try to keep a “clean” image while using huge amounts of resources. This gap between marketing and reality is hard for buyers to navigate. The upside is that tech optimism brings investment in efficient digital tools. The downside is the hidden environmental cost of our digital lives. If consumers ignore these costs, they support a system that values speed over sustainability.

Regulators are starting to fix these problems. The European Union passed a rule requiring proof for all environmental claims. In the US, the Federal Trade Commission is updating its rules to challenge fake green goals. For example, Coca-Cola now faces legal trouble for hiding its plastic pollution. This change is good for consumers. It offers more transparency. Buyers can trust data instead of clever slogans. However, there are downsides. Strict rules might raise prices. Also, it is hard for consumers to check claims in complex supply chains, like those for coffee and chocolate.

Consumers can protect themselves by checking if a brand’s actions match its ads. Research shows that shoppers spot “greenwashing” when they see “claim stacking.” This is when companies pile on vague, unproven statements. To avoid being tricked, shoppers should demand specific details about environmental impact. Supporting brands that match their words with real actions helps keep the market honest. Using shared industry standards ensures that money supports real facts. By demanding truth, consumers can see through the illusion and choose only what is truly good for the planet.

One year of Project Green: A new hope for open spaces or a green washing exercise?
The Maltese government’s flagship initiative, Project Green, faces accusations of greenwashing due to a significant disconnect between its ambitious electoral promises and its actual outcomes. While the Labour Party campaigned on creating expansive new green urban spaces, parliamentary data reveals that the vast majority of the agency’s recent work involves rehabilitating valleys and upgrading existing gardens. Only a small fraction of projects have resulted in genuinely new public open spaces, suggesting that the initiative focuses more on minor enhancements than the transformative environmental change originally promised to voters. This discrepancy is exacerbated by simultaneous government actions that undermine environmental conservation efforts. Critics highlight that while the agency markets these small-scale improvements, it has also permitted the development or sale of public lands hosting indigenous flora and natural habitats. The contrast between investing in aesthetic upgrades for existing spaces and allowing the destruction of valuable unbuilt land creates a narrative inconsistency. This dual approach allows the government to claim progress in greening while continuing policies that facilitate urban sprawl, leading to concerns that the initiative serves more as a public relations tool than a substantive environmental strategy. The relevance to greenwashing lies in the deliberate mismatch between rhetoric and reality. By heavily promoting minor revamps and valley cleanups as major ecological achievements, the agency creates an illusion of rapid environmental improvement. However, the allowance of nearby destructive developments, such as large-scale construction on agricultural land, negates these local benefits. Consequently, Project Green risks being labeled a greenwashing entity because it prioritizes appearance over substance, failing to address the broader imbalance between rampant development and genuine environmental preservation.

PSC chair laments ‘greenwashing’ through North Dakota wind power
North Dakota regulators are raising alarms about a proposed wind farm project where Verizon, a corporation with no actual energy needs, contracts to purchase electricity solely to meet environmental goals. This arrangement is highlighted as a textbook example of greenwashing, where companies invest in renewable energy not to reduce their own carbon footprint or address genuine demand, but to manufacture an eco-friendly image that deflects criticism from environmental activists. The regulatory concern extends beyond marketing optics to tangible market impacts. By securing government-subsidized power under these artificial contracts, the project risks displacing traditional, non-subsidized energy sources on the grid. This dynamic threatens the economic viability of existing local infrastructure, such as coal plants, potentially leading to premature closures and destabilizing the energy market, all while providing no real-world benefit to local consumers who do not require the additional capacity. This case is critically relevant to the discourse on greenwashing because it exposes the disconnect between corporate sustainability claims and actual operational necessity. It illustrates how financial incentives and public perception can drive renewable energy investments that serve primarily as PR tools rather than genuine ecological solutions. Consequently, it underscores the importance of scrutinizing renewable energy contracts to ensure they reflect true energy demand rather than just corporate branding strategies.

The greenwashing trap: how green hydrogen can keep its promises
The article highlights how ambiguous terminology in the hydrogen industry facilitates greenwashing, particularly when governments label carbon-intensive blue hydrogen as "low-carbon." This misleading classification exploits public optimism for clean energy while continuing to rely on fossil fuels and unproven carbon capture technologies. The core issue lies in the absence of strict definitions, allowing companies to market dirty hydrogen under the eco-friendly banner of green hydrogen, thereby undermining genuine environmental progress. A critical driver of this problem is the lack of robust, globally standardized emission thresholds for green hydrogen. Without mandatory limits, voluntary certifications and vague ISO standards fail to distinguish truly clean fuel from dirty alternatives, effectively acting as tools for greenwashing rather than protection against it. Recent regulatory efforts, such as the EU’s introduction of "additionality" requirements, attempt to close these loopholes by ensuring green hydrogen production is directly tied to new renewable energy sources. These measures underscore the urgent need for stringent policies that prevent the dilution of green claims through insufficient oversight. To combat skepticism and ensure transparency, the industry must adopt immutable tracking technologies like blockchain to verify emissions data at every stage of production. While technological solutions offer a promising path toward authentic traceability, they are only effective when paired with strong policy frameworks that define and enforce what constitutes green hydrogen. Ultimately, clarity and bold, accurate standards are essential to restore public trust and prevent the clean hydrogen revolution from being sabotaged by deceptive marketing practices.
