Complaint filed against oilsands alliance over ‘false and misleading’ claims
Greenpeace Canada has formally requested an investigation by the Competition Bureau into the Pathways Alliance, accusing the group of greenwashing through its “net-zero” advertising campaign. The complaint argues that the alliance of major oil producers is misleading the public by claiming climate neutrality while their actual emissions continue to rise and their investments in clean technology remain minimal. This campaign is viewed as an attempt to manufacture political support for continued fossil fuel extraction by falsely positioning their oil as environmentally superior to other producers. The core of the allegation lies in the selective accounting of greenhouse gas emissions. The Pathways Alliance’s net-zero claims consider only operational emissions from extraction and processing, deliberately excluding the vast majority of pollution generated when their products are ultimately burned by consumers. By omitting these downstream impacts, the alliance creates a deceptive impression of sustainability. Regulatory guidelines require environmental claims to account for a product’s entire lifecycle, meaning the exclusion of these significant emissions renders the advertising false and misleading under Canadian competition law. This case highlights the growing regulatory scrutiny of corporate environmental marketing. By clarifying on their website that their goals apply only to upstream emissions, the alliance effectively admits to the loophole but continues to rely on broad public advertising that obscures this limitation. The complaint underscores the tension between industry self-reporting and consumer protection, signaling that regulators are increasingly prioritizing the enforcement of accurate lifecycle assessments to prevent companies from gaining unfair market advantages through exaggerated green claims.

Compliance & Risks Reveals the Latest in Apparel Regulatory Trends
The apparel industry faces intensifying regulatory pressure regarding sustainability, with extended producer responsibility and ESG mandates becoming central compliance concerns. Regulators are actively updating measures to minimize environmental impacts, reflecting a global shift toward greater ecological accountability within the textile sector. This regulatory evolution directly highlights the prevalence of greenwashing, as brands must now rigorously substantiate their environmental claims to meet legal standards. The emphasis on precise labelling and transparent sustainability strategies serves as a countermeasure to misleading marketing, ensuring that corporate environmental assertions align with verifiable data and consumer expectations. Understanding these shifting compliance landscapes is crucial for discerning genuine sustainability efforts from deceptive practices. As authorities tighten rules on eco-claims, companies risk significant reputational damage if they fail to align operations with actual environmental performance, making regulatory adherence the primary defense against accusations of greenwashing.

Sizewell C: Chancellor Jeremy Hunt's 'greenwashing' slammed
The UK government’s classification of nuclear energy alongside renewables for investment incentives raises greenwashing concerns by equating distinct technologies. This move ignores the critical scientific reality of radioactive waste, potentially misleading investors and the public about nuclear’s environmental sustainability. Critics argue that linking civil and defense nuclear enterprises obscures true motivations, while financial risks remain high for projects like Sizewell C. The lack of transparency regarding the Great British Nuclear scheme’s actual capacity suggests the initiative may be more about political signaling than delivering viable, clean energy solutions. This announcement is relevant to greenwashing as it rebrands a high-waste energy source as eco-friendly. By treating nuclear as equivalent to renewables, the policy risks diluting the definition of "green" investment, allowing governments to claim climate progress while continuing to support infrastructure that contradicts fundamental ecological science.

World must act now to end carbon offset scam
An investigation revealed that the vast majority of rainforest offset credits certified by Verra, a dominant industry certifier, fail to represent genuine carbon reductions. In response to these severe integrity gaps, Verra announced plans to scrap its current system by 2025, acknowledging the need to prevent misuse in the multi-billion-dollar market. This scandal highlights the critical unreliability of current certification mechanisms, which are often devoid of rigorous verification. The carbon offset industry has long been criticized for allowing major polluters to essentially buy their way out of accountability rather than reducing emissions at the source. By financing environmental projects in distant regions, corporations create a façade of sustainability while continuing to operate their high-pollution operations. This voluntary, unregulated framework functions as a lucrative revenue stream for businesses, enabling them to market carbon-neutral products to consumers who lack the means to verify the actual environmental impact of their contributions. This situation is highly relevant to greenwashing because it demonstrates how sophisticated financial mechanisms are used to deceive the public. Companies exploit the lack of government oversight and consumer awareness to sell the illusion of climate action through flawed accounting methods. Without immediate regulatory intervention to establish real, verifiable standards, the offset system risks becoming a vehicle for larger-scale fraud, allowing corporations to continue polluting while claiming ethical responsibility.
