EU should not legally define ‘greenwashing,’ fund groups say
Financial industry groups strongly oppose the legal definition of greenwashing, arguing that codifying the term creates unnecessary complexity in a rapidly evolving sector. They contend that establishing a rigid statutory definition is counterproductive, as it may hinder regulatory flexibility. Instead, these associations recommend focusing on describing specific misleading conduct rather than creating new legal categories that might detach from existing comprehensive rules. These organizations emphasize that asset managers rely heavily on third-party data and ESG ratings, often beyond their direct control. Consequently, they warn against applying the label too broadly, noting that sustainability claims can be inadvertently misleading without necessarily constituting fraud. This perspective highlights the difficulty in distinguishing between negligent errors and deliberate deception, suggesting that current frameworks should be utilized to address inconsistencies rather than introducing additional legal burdens. This debate is critical to understanding greenwashing because it reveals the tension between regulatory clarity and industry resistance. While regulators seek precise definitions to enforce sanctions and protect investors, the industry argues for nuanced enforcement based on specific actions. This dynamic shapes how greenwashing is policed globally, influencing whether accountability arises from broad statutory definitions or targeted supervisory actions based on actual market conduct and disclosure failures.

It’s time to move beyond ESG bashing
The article argues that stringent regulatory efforts to combat greenwashing by narrowly defining "sustainable" investments may ultimately hinder the transition to a greener economy. By restricting terms like "green" or "ESG" only to funds with demonstrable positive impacts, regulators risk excluding broad strategies that systematically manage environmental risks. This bifurcation ignores the reality that risk mitigation and positive impact are often intertwined, meaning that responsible risk management contributes significantly to long-term sustainability goals. Consequently, such narrow definitions could distort capital markets by directing too much investment into a limited pool of already-qualified assets, potentially causing them to become overvalued while overlooking other sectors with high potential for impact. The author suggests that this hyper-specific approach treats regulatory perfection as the enemy of progress. Instead of creating a small niche of compliant products, regulations should aim to make sustainability the standard practice across the entire financial industry. This perspective is crucial to understanding greenwashing because it highlights a counterproductive side of anti-greenwashing measures. While transparency is necessary, excessive scrutiny that penalizes incremental improvement can stifle the broad behavioral changes needed to scale sustainable finance. The article emphasizes that the true goal should be shifting the mindset of the majority of market participants, rather than creating rigid categories that exclude significant portions of responsible investing.

This topic is highly relevant to greenwashing because it addresses the core challenge of defining and supervising deceptive practices in sustainable finance.
