ASIC sues Mercer Super in greenwashing crackdown
The Australian Securities and Investments Commission has initiated its first legal proceedings against Mercer Superannuation for alleged greenwashing, signaling a significant escalation in regulatory enforcement. By taking a major industry player to court, ASIC aims to deter misleading marketing practices where financial products claim strong environmental credentials while simultaneously investing in excluded sectors like fossil fuels, alcohol, and gambling. This high-profile case demonstrates that regulators are no longer issuing mere warnings but are prepared to seek substantial penalties and injunctions to enforce truthfulness in sustainability claims. The core issue revolves around the discrepancy between marketed exclusions and actual portfolio holdings, highlighting how easily consumer trust can be eroded by vague or false ethical branding. Mercer’s “Sustainable Plus” options promised exclusions of carbon-intensive industries, yet members were found invested in companies explicitly banned by those promises. This disconnect illustrates the dangerous reality of greenwashing, where the allure of sustainable investing is exploited without corresponding ethical standards, leaving investors misled about where their money truly goes. This action is highly relevant to greenwashing discussions because it establishes a clear precedent that subjective ESG labels must be substantiated by verifiable data. Rather than debating the definition of sustainability, regulators are focusing strictly on disclosure accuracy, forcing companies to ensure their marketing aligns perfectly with their investments. As more funds face potential scrutiny, this case sends a stern warning to the financial industry that greenwashing will be met with aggressive legal consequences, necessitating greater transparency and accountability in how sustainable products are presented to the public.

Letter to the editor: Hydropower is not clean energy
The article argues that Central Maine Power is greenwashing by labeling the New England Clean Energy Connect as clean. This marketing spin deceptively frames hydroelectric power from Quebec as environmentally friendly, masking the true motive of corporate financial gain behind ecological benefits. Hydropower is misrepresented as emissions-free, despite reservoirs emitting significant methane. Recent U.N. reporting standards now require dam operators to disclose these emissions, challenging the validity of hydro as a sustainable energy source. Ignoring this data leads to inaccurate permitting and misguided infrastructure investments. This case is relevant to greenwashing because it illustrates how corporations exploit outdated definitions of "clean" energy to justify environmentally harmful projects. The piece urges the public to scrutinize such claims and oppose the corridor, highlighting the danger of accepting corporate narratives without verifying scientific facts.
