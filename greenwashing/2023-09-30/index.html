Asset managers tread carefully following DWS’s fine by SEC
The recent substantial fines imposed on major asset managers like DWS underscore a critical shift in the regulatory landscape, where misleading ESG marketing is now treated with severe enforcement rigor. This trend highlights that regulators are moving beyond mere oversight to actively prosecuting discrepancies between a firm’s public sustainability claims and its actual operational controls. Consequently, the financial and legal risks associated with greenwashing have escalated, forcing institutions to prioritize strict adherence to both the letter and the spirit of the law to avoid catastrophic reputational and monetary damages. The definition of greenwashing is expanding to include unintentional omissions, making transparency the primary defense for asset managers. Industry leaders emphasize that simply following existing regulations is insufficient; firms must clearly articulate their investment strategies and avoid overpromising, particularly when distinguishing between financially material ESG factors and broader environmental impacts. This heightened scrutiny demands that companies align their internal governance and product classifications with investor expectations, ensuring that labels accurately reflect the true nature of the investments rather than serving as mere marketing tools. Ultimately, the proliferation of complex and sometimes divergent global regulations presents a significant challenge for maintaining consistency and credibility. Asset managers must navigate a fragmented landscape of disclosures and labeling regimes, which increases the likelihood of misinterpretation and subsequent litigation. To mitigate these risks, the industry is compelled to adopt robust governance frameworks and proactive transparency measures, ensuring that sustainability claims are substantiated by clear, verifiable data to protect investor trust and maintain market integrity.

Climate models underestimate renewables progress, overstate net zero costs: study
The study reveals that conventional climate models have systematically underestimated the rapid cost declines and adoption rates of solar power and energy storage, suggesting a net-zero transition is more feasible than previously assumed. By ignoring the accelerated learning curves of granular technologies, these models continue to favor inefficient combustion methods, creating a significant gap between projected climate pathways and technological reality. This discrepancy highlights a critical failure in how future scenarios are constructed, as they fail to capture the true potential of intermittent renewables and efficient end-use solutions. This underestimation is particularly relevant to greenwashing because it allows industries and policymakers to justify slow progress or continued reliance on fossil fuels based on outdated data. When official models claim that decarbonization is prohibitively expensive or technically difficult, it creates a narrative that supports incremental, often insufficient, climate actions. By not accounting for the dramatic fall in costs and the speed of innovation, these models provide a misleading baseline that can be exploited to delay necessary systemic changes, thereby preserving the status quo for carbon-intensive sectors under the guise of scientific caution. Ultimately, the article argues that current climate science must urgently update its assumptions to reflect real-world economic and technological dynamics. Failing to do so risks producing policy guidance that is disconnected from the actual capabilities of clean energy markets. For stakeholders, this means that the barrier to a rapid transition is less about technical impossibility or high cost, and more about political will and infrastructure challenges. Recognizing this shift is essential to distinguishing genuine climate action from empty promises, ensuring that strategic decisions are based on the actual viability of renewable solutions rather than conservative, outdated forecasts.

Privacy washing: Google claims to support privacy while lobbying against it | Proton
Google’s public advocacy for privacy stands in stark contrast to its aggressive lobbying efforts to undermine regulations that would restrict data surveillance. By spending millions to influence policymakers in the US, Europe, and beyond, the company actively seeks to water down or stall legislation designed to protect consumer data. This behavior reveals a fundamental hypocrisy where marketing claims of privacy concern clash with actions aimed at preserving the lucrative targeted advertising business model that relies on extensive data collection. This dynamic mirrors the concept of greenwashing, here termed “privacy washing,” where companies create an eco-conscious or privacy-friendly image to avoid scrutiny while continuing harmful practices. Just as polluters market themselves as sustainable to deflect environmental criticism, Google positions itself as a guardian of user rights while simultaneously fighting laws that limit its ability to exploit personal information. The article argues that true privacy cannot exist within a business structure dependent on intimate data profiling, making Google’s public stance a strategic distraction rather than a genuine commitment to user safety. The relevance to greenwashing lies in exposing this deliberate misalignment between corporate narrative and operational reality. By identifying “privacy washing” as a direct analogue to environmental deception, the article highlights how Big Tech manipulates public perception to maintain dominance. It urges consumers to look beyond marketing rhetoric and evaluate companies based on their actual political and business practices, suggesting that relying on regulatory changes alone is insufficient without broader shifts toward privacy-first alternatives.

S&Ds lead the way to ban greenwashing in the EU | Socialists & Democrats
The EU has reached a political agreement to empower consumers during the green transition by strictly regulating environmental marketing. This new law aims to eliminate misleading green claims, ensuring that products labeled as sustainable actually meet rigorous environmental standards. By requiring proof for generic terms and banning vague slogans, the legislation seeks to restore trust in eco-labels and prevent companies from deceiving buyers with unsubstantiated environmental benefits. A crucial aspect of this reform is the prohibition of claims based on carbon offsetting, such as “climate neutral,” unless backed by recognized performance. This directly targets common greenwashing tactics where companies mask their environmental footprint through compensatory measures rather than genuine reduction. The agreement also addresses early obsolescence by banning communications that highlight features designed to limit a product’s durability, thereby promoting truly long-lasting and repairable goods. Furthermore, the deal enhances transparency by mandating clear reminders of legal guarantees on packaging and in shops. This ensures consumers are aware of their rights to durability and can make informed choices based on actual product longevity. These measures are highly relevant to greenwashing as they establish a legal framework to distinguish authentic sustainability from marketing hype, ultimately forcing companies to align their commercial practices with scientific evidence and genuine ecological responsibility.
