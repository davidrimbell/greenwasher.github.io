Glenn McGrath’s family farm and a stoush over carbon policy ‘scam’

The article highlights a significant controversy surrounding Human Induced Regeneration (HIR), a carbon credit scheme where farmers receive financial incentives for allowing natural forest regeneration by reducing grazing. Ecologists argue that this method is fundamentally flawed because satellite data and field inspections reveal negligible increases in tree cover, suggesting that rainfall, not grazing management, drives vegetation cycles in these arid landscapes. Consequently, the carbon credits issued under HIR likely do not represent genuine, additional carbon storage, as the forests would have grown regardless of the intervention. This discrepancy raises serious greenwashing concerns, as major fossil fuel companies purchase these questionable offsets to claim emission reductions without making substantial cuts to their own pollution. The study indicates that companies can buy vast quantities of low-integrity credits, allowing them to maintain business-as-usual operations while marketing themselves as environmentally responsible. Critics warn that this system allows polluters to buy their way out of accountability, creating a market for cheap offsets that undermine genuine climate action. Furthermore, there are fears that proposed legislative changes will enable these potentially fraudulent projects to continue operating under new, weaker regulations rather than facing penalties for failure. This regulatory shift could institutionalize the issuance of credits that lack ecological basis, effectively protecting the financial interests of project developers at the expense of environmental integrity. The situation underscores how carbon markets can be exploited to generate revenue from non-existent environmental benefits, distorting the true cost of industrial emissions.
