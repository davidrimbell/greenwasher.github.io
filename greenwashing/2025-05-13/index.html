Beware Fake Eco Labels: How Greenwashing Tricks Shoppers and How to Spot It

Companies use a trick called “greenwashing.” This happens when they make false claims about being eco-friendly. This trick works during the early stages of shopping. It helps brands look good but hurts buyers.

Companies get attention by putting green labels on their products. This attracts shoppers who care about the planet. For example, a study by the University of Queensland (https://www.uq.edu.au) shows that people think green-labeled items are higher quality. This is true even if the facts say otherwise. Because of this, brands can charge more money. They do this without actually changing how they make things.

However, this strategy has bad results. Buyers can lose money or face health risks. Shoppers who trust these fake labels may buy poor-quality items. They might also buy things that do not match their values. Research in the Journal of Consumer Policy (https://link.springer.com/journal/10877) shows that people often regret these purchases. This makes them trust brands less.

Some cases show real health dangers. In Malta, the EcoHive project (https://www.ecohive.com.mt) hid industrial waste as a community project. This exposed residents to harmful pollution. Local experts warn about dangerous PM2.5 particles in the air.

Now, governments are stepping in to help. France has banned ads for fossil fuels. They also limit vague claims like “carbon neutral.” Brands must now prove their green claims with hard evidence. This helps consumers get the truth, not just marketing tricks. The EU’s Green Claims Directive (https://environment.ec.europa.eu/topics/circular-economy/green-claims_en) also requires clear information. This rule aims to remove confusion about sustainability. These laws help shoppers make smart choices.

True sustainability needs honesty, not just clever ads. Some brands, like Untouched World (https://www.untouchedworld.com), show true integrity. This company, founded by Peri Drysdale, focuses on recycling and keeping waste out of landfills. This is very different from large brands that make unrecyclable clothes and call them “sustainable.”

When you support ethical companies, you help change the industry. The Fashion Reimagined programme (https://www.fashionreimagined.org) teaches new designers to avoid these tricks. It builds a culture of honesty. If you are shopping, look closely at claims. Check for proof. Support brands that walk the talk. This protects your wallet and health. It also helps the planet.


France has initiated a significant regulatory shift against greenwashing, becoming the first nation to ban fossil fuel advertising and restricting vague sustainability claims like "carbon neutral." By mandating transparent disclosure of greenhouse gas emissions and requiring rigorous evidence for environmental assertions, the country is establishing a precedent that challenges the ad industry to move beyond superficial messaging. This legislative approach introduces substantial financial penalties for non-compliance, signaling that business-as-usual in sustainable marketing is no longer viable. The relevance to greenwashing lies in the contrast between France’s strict, enforceable standards and the often voluntary or reactive measures seen elsewhere, such as in the UK. While other regions rely on consumer protection laws that only activate upon third-party legal action, France proactively demands public accountability and adherence to specific environmental standards. This highlights the danger of ambiguous terminology and proves that legislative frameworks are becoming the primary tool for dismantling deceptive environmental marketing practices. Ultimately, these regulations compel brands to integrate genuine sustainability into their core operations rather than treating it as a mere communication strategy. The implication is that companies must back their marketing claims with verifiable, comprehensive action, fostering a broader cultural shift toward authentic environmental responsibility. As this tide turns across the EU, advertisers face the urgent need to develop competence in authentic sustainability, ensuring that their contributions to greener industries are substantial rather than just rhetorical.

‘EcoHive project has strong element of greenwashing,’ PN MP raises alarm over EcoHive incinerators - The Malta Independent
The article highlights how the Maltese government’s EcoHive project is accused of significant greenwashing by disguising a major industrial waste complex as an environmentally friendly initiative. Politician Claudette Buttigieg argues that branding incinerators and hazardous waste facilities as a “family park” misleads the public into believing the project is sustainable. This deceptive marketing obscures the reality that the site will house multiple incinerators, including those for toxic and hospital waste, thereby exploiting positive environmental terminology to justify a highly polluting industrial expansion. Central to the criticism is the severe public health risk posed by bundling various waste treatment facilities together. Residents and officials question the validity of isolated impact assessments, arguing that the cumulative effect of two incinerators and other plants creates a significant pollution threat. The project’s location, combined with prevailing wind patterns, risks blowing harmful particulates over densely populated coastal areas. The omission of data on finer PM2.5 particles further exacerbates concerns, as these microscopic toxins can enter the bloodstream, posing long-term health dangers that the current transparency measures fail to adequately address. This case is critically relevant to the broader discourse on greenwashing because it exemplifies the tension between corporate environmental claims and actual ecological impact. It underscores why frameworks like the EU’s Green Claims Directive are essential, aiming to prevent organizations from misleading consumers about their environmental benefits. The article serves as a cautionary tale about the necessity of rigorous, holistic impact assessments and full public consultation. It demonstrates that without strict regulatory oversight and transparency, the “eco-friendly” label can be weaponized to advance projects that fundamentally contradict genuine sustainability goals, prioritizing economic interests over community well-being.

Designer Peri Drysdale isn't here to greenwash, she's been walking the talk for 40 years
The article positions Untouched World as a definitive counter-example to greenwashing, contrasting its decades-long, holistic sustainability practices against the superficial marketing tactics prevalent in the fashion industry. Founder Peri Drysdale criticizes the widespread miscommunication and false claims made by larger brands that prioritize newsworthy sustainability narratives over genuine environmental impact. This distinction is crucial because it highlights how true sustainability requires tangible actions, such as designing for recyclability, rather than vague or misleading assertions that confuse consumers. A primary illustration of this commitment is the brand’s rigorous approach to material innovation and waste management. Unlike competitors that create unrecyclable fiber blends for marketing purposes, Untouched World actively diverts nearly all textile waste from landfills and invests in circular solutions like recycled yarns. By focusing on durability and end-of-life recyclability, the brand demonstrates that environmental responsibility involves practical problem-solving and transparency, directly addressing the pitfalls of greenwashing where products are labeled "eco-friendly" despite lacking structural sustainability. Furthermore, the narrative emphasizes the importance of educating the next generation of designers to combat industry-wide deception. Through initiatives like the Fashion Reimagined programme, the brand mentors students to prioritize quality and ethical manufacturing over fleeting trends. This educational role is relevant to the greenwashing discourse because it fosters a future leadership capable of recognizing and rejecting superficial sustainability claims, thereby promoting a fashion culture rooted in integrity, long-term thinking, and authentic environmental stewardship.
