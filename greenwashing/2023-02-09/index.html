National Park Service accused of failing to protect endangered Florida wildlife
Environmental groups are suing the National Park Service and the Department of the Interior, alleging that the agencies improperly removed land-use restrictions to allow a large-scale development project to proceed without mandated environmental reviews. By releasing the land from its requirement to remain a public park, the government effectively bypassed protections under the Endangered Species Act and the National Environmental Policy Act. This legal maneuver permits construction to begin before assessing the potential harm to the local ecosystem, setting a dangerous precedent for how federal lands are managed and protected. The lawsuit highlights the severe threat the proposed water park and retail complex poses to critically endangered species and the unique pine rocklands habitat. Conservationists argue that the development will destroy vital foraging and roosting grounds for rare animals, including the Florida bonneted bat and specific butterfly species, while fragmenting one of the last remaining large areas of this biodiverse ecosystem. The destruction of such irreplaceable habitats undermines global conservation efforts, as these specific ecosystems are already critically diminished and struggle to survive amidst climate change and urban expansion. This case is highly relevant to greenwashing because the developer markets the project as "eco-sensitive," a claim directly contradicted by the allegation that it violates core environmental laws. The discrepancy between the developer’s sustainability branding and the legal reality of bypassing endangered species protections illustrates how commercial interests may use superficial green narratives to obscure significant ecological damage. It exposes the risk that "eco-friendly" labels can be deployed to legitimize projects that actually accelerate habitat loss and species extinction.

Resisting neoliberal greenwashing in Dutch universities
The University of Amsterdam exemplifies institutional greenwashing by maintaining lucrative partnerships with fossil fuel giants like Shell while publicly claiming a commitment to sustainability. This hypocrisy is highlighted by the university’s refusal to sever ties with the oil industry, despite Shell’s vast investments in carbon-intensive projects. By framing these collaborations as contributions to sustainable futures, the institution masks its role in perpetuating environmental degradation and capitalizes on the moral authority of academia to legitimize ecologically destructive practices. This environmental complicity is further entangled with colonial violence through the university’s opaque relationships with settler-colonial institutions. The refusal to fully disclose academic agreements with entities linked to the occupation of Palestinian land reveals a pattern of hiding complicity in human rights abuses. This lack of transparency suggests that the university prioritizes maintaining connections with powerful, controversial global structures over ethical accountability, using the guise of academic freedom to shield itself from scrutiny regarding its colonial ties. The repression of student activists demanding decarbonization and decolonization exposes the neoliberal mechanisms used to enforce this status quo. By deploying police force against peaceful protesters, the university demonstrates that its democratic ideals are conditional, serving only to protect corporate interests and institutional stability. This violent suppression underscores how greenwashing functions not just as marketing, but as a tool to silence dissent and maintain a system that simultaneously exploits the environment and oppressed communities under the banner of progress.
