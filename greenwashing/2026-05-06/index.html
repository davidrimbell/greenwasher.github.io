'Peak Cluster is not about greenwashing or ticking boxes', says pipeline chief
The article highlights a sharp conflict between the Peak Cluster project and local stakeholders, who accuse the initiative of being a deceptive environmental facade rather than a genuine sustainability effort. Community leaders and activists characterize the project as industrial-scale greenwashing, arguing that it prioritizes foreign investor profits over local well-being by disrupting the green belt and treating public consultation as a mere procedural formality. This backlash underscores the tension between large-scale infrastructure claims and community trust, revealing how environmental narratives can be perceived as manipulative when local voices feel ignored. In response, project leadership defends the initiative as essential for securing a reliable, domestic supply of cement and lime, which are critical for the UK’s construction and defense sectors. They argue that relying on imports exposes the nation to economic volatility and supply chain disruptions, positioning the project as a necessary step for national security and economic stability. By emphasizing the creation of skilled British jobs and the prevention of increased carbon imports, the proponents attempt to reframe the narrative from environmental exploitation to national industrial resilience. Ultimately, the dispute centers on whether carbon capture technology constitutes a legitimate climate solution or a pretext for maintaining polluting industries. The relevance to greenwashing lies in this ambiguity: critics see it as a way to continue fossil-fuel-adjacent production under the guise of neutrality, while supporters view it as a pragmatic adaptation for a net-zero future. This case illustrates the critical importance of transparent community engagement and verifying the actual environmental benefits of such projects to prevent the misuse of sustainability claims for commercial gain.

ArcelorMittal: emisiones tóxicas y acusaciones de ‘greenwashing’ en su informe
ArcelorMittal is at the center of a controversy over alleged greenwashing, as its public sustainability discourse clashes with its tangible environmental impact. Critics argue that the company’s sustainability reports are vague and overly reliant on external variables, such as energy prices, to justify delays in climate action. This discrepancy raises serious questions about corporate credibility, suggesting that the steelmaker is prioritizing strategic posturing over the immediate, structural changes necessary for genuine decarbonization. The situation is exacerbated by the company’s reliance on public subsidies and free emission permits, which ease financial pressure and reduce the urgency for industrial transformation. Despite these benefits and rising profits, the firm continues to delay critical investments in cleaning up its production processes. As a result, ArcelorMittal remains one of Spain’s largest polluters, with its facilities in Asturias emitting toxic substances that pose significant health risks to local populations, including chronic respiratory problems, while the promised environmental improvements remain insufficient. This case highlights the broader challenge of aligning corporate rhetoric with operational reality during the energy transition. The accusation of greenwashing stems from marketing steel as a key material for green technologies while maintaining a high-carbon production model. Ultimately, the debate underscores the need for a just transition that not only achieves real ecological reductions but also addresses social concerns, ensuring that industrial transformation does not come at the cost of public health or jobs.

Eco-tourist attractions and greenwashing: What you can do
The article warns that the booming eco-tourism industry is frequently plagued by greenwashing, where attractions market themselves as environmentally responsible while engaging in harmful practices. Using a specific sea turtle facility as a primary example, the text highlights the critical discrepancy between marketing claims and actual operations, demonstrating how easily tourists can be misled by superficial "eco-friendly" branding that masks exploitation and poor animal welfare standards. Key indicators of deceptive eco-tourism include encouraging direct physical contact with wildlife, prioritizing entertainment over genuine scientific conservation, and selling protected species for food or other commercial purposes. These practices not only traumatize the animals and compromise their health but also create direct conflicts of interest that undermine the core principles of conservation. Furthermore, relying on trusted third-party reviews and questioning travel providers can expose these contradictions, as reputable organizations often flag attractions that fail to meet ethical industry standards. This content is highly relevant to greenwashing because it provides a practical framework for identifying false environmental claims in the tourism sector. It illustrates how companies exploit the public’s desire to make ethical choices by using vague terminology and engaging in deceptive marketing. By empowering consumers to scrutinize business models rather than accepting surface-level sustainability narratives, the article exposes the structural mechanisms that allow greenwashing to persist, urging a shift from passive consumption to active verification of ethical practices.

Norwegian government rebuked over decision to reopen North Sea gas fields
The Norwegian government’s decision to approve new oil and gas exploration and reopen dormant North Sea fields highlights a stark contradiction between climate rhetoric and energy policy. By prioritizing immediate European energy security and domestic economic benefits over environmental advice, the administration is accused of engaging in greenwashing. Critics argue that labeling these expansion plans as "responsible" extraction is a deceptive narrative designed to mask the increased environmental risks and carbon emissions associated with fossil fuel dependency. This move underscores how geopolitical crises, such as the conflict in the Middle East, are being used to justify the continuation and expansion of hydrocarbon production. The government’s stance directly opposes warnings from its own environment agency, revealing a systemic disconnect where political expediency overrides scientific consensus. The narrative of supporting welfare and safety is increasingly viewed as a smokescreen for sustaining the fossil fuel industry, effectively using the energy crisis to legitimize long-term environmental degradation. The relevance to greenwashing lies in the deliberate framing of expanded oil and gas activities as essential for a green transition. By claiming that Norwegian supplies are a lesser evil compared to other global sources, the state attempts to sanitize the climate impact of its actions. This article demonstrates how governments can exploit urgent energy shortages to dilute environmental commitments, presenting harmful industrial expansion as a necessary, eco-conscious choice to maintain public support while ignoring catastrophic ecological consequences.
