Why vague eco-labels and greenhushing hurt shoppers

Shoppers in the early stages of buying often find it hard to understand sustainability claims. Companies use this confusion to sell more products. This creates both good and bad situations for buyers.

In Australia, organic labels do not have strict legal rules. This lets businesses sell products with very few real organic ingredients. This helps companies save money and make more profit. However, it hurts shoppers. They pay high prices for items that are not truly ethical or eco-friendly. A 2023 investigation by the Australian Competition and Consumer Commission (ACCC) found many cases where stores used vague words to trick customers about where products came from.

Banks and financial groups also face these challenges. In 2023, a US court ruled that American Airlines broke its duty to shareholders. The airline chose BlackRock as its retirement fund manager because of ESG (Environmental, Social, and Governance) rules. This ruling helps shareholders who want quick money rather than long-term sustainability. But it risks weakening important risk management. Asset managers might stop caring about climate change to avoid lawsuits. This could put investments at greater risk.

The farming industry has similar problems. The term “regenerative” farming has no single definition. This allows companies to make unproven claims about fixing the land. It helps them stand out in a crowded market without paying for strict checks. Shoppers think they are helping the environment. But they often get only small changes that do not really improve soil health or wildlife. A 2022 study by the University of California, Davis, showed that many “regenerative” products lacked proof of their environmental impact.

This uncertainty causes a problem called “greenhushing” in Spain. Companies hide their real green efforts to avoid being accused of greenwashing. This silence helps firms avoid public anger and legal trouble. But it hurts shoppers. They lose access to information about truly eco-friendly choices. A 2023 European Commission report said this lack of honesty makes people cynical. It reduces trust in all corporate green claims. Now, the EU requires strong proof for such claims. This forces companies to be either transparent or silent.

Shoppers must think carefully about these issues. Clear rules that close loopholes can help. At the same time, there is a risk of supporting companies that care more about marketing than the planet. By asking for real data and supporting strict rules, buyers can make better choices. This approach ensures that your purchases help the actual environment, not just superficial branding.
