Environmentalists warn that the Senate’s carbon credit bill puts ecosystems and climate goals at risk

Argentina’s new carbon credit regulations have sparked widespread backlash by allowing companies to offset their pollution without reducing it at the source. By granting tax benefits and prioritizing the commercialization of these assets, there is a risk that major polluters will continue emitting greenhouse gases while financing capture projects in other territories. This dynamic turns environmental protection into a financial commodity, subordinates ecosystem conservation to the generation of credits, and undermines the rights of indigenous and peasant communities in the face of speculative interests. The implementation of this market raises serious technical and ethical concerns, as the complexity of verifying the integrity of emission reductions opens the door to corruption and the laundering of illegal activities such as deforestation. Experts warn of the danger of selling certificates that do not represent real emission reductions—so-called “greenwashing” or “green smoke”—which distances the country from meeting its national climate targets. By merging voluntary markets with weak regulations, the system incentivizes land accumulation and the creation of unambitious climate targets to generate commercial surpluses rather than achieving a genuine ecological transition. This article is crucial for understanding greenwashing because it illustrates how legal structures can legitimize climate inaction under the guise of sustainability. By facilitating the purchase of credits without requiring effective reductions in corporate emissions, companies can continue their polluting practices while maintaining a misleadingly eco-friendly image. This practice diverts attention from the structural transformations needed and exploits deficient regulatory frameworks to monetize inertia, demonstrating that carbon trading, without strict safeguards, becomes another tool for false decarbonization rather than real mitigation.
