Carbon offsetting: Meet the man behind the curtain of your favourite ‘carbon-neutral’ brands

The article highlights the critical tension between corporate claims of carbon neutrality and the actual efficacy of carbon offsetting, particularly within nature-based solutions. It reveals that a significant portion of offset projects, such as REDD initiatives, fail to deliver on their projected deforestation reduction goals. This widespread underperformance has eroded trust in the voluntary carbon market, allowing companies to effectively "pay-to-play" pollution rather than engaging in genuine emission reductions. By masking continued high emissions behind purchased credits, corporations risk engaging in greenwashing, creating a facade of sustainability without addressing the root cause of their environmental impact. To address these credibility gaps, researchers have developed the PACT framework, an open-source system designed to enhance transparency and accuracy in carbon credit verification. Unlike previous methods, PACT utilizes long-term monitoring and dynamic credit issuance to correct for over- or underestimations of a project’s effectiveness. This approach aims to restore investor confidence by providing precise, comparable metrics for diverse carbon projects. However, while PACT improves the reliability of impact assessments, it does not resolve fundamental issues regarding the conceptualization or implementation flaws of the projects themselves, nor does it guarantee that reduced deforestation stems from genuine conservation efforts rather than other factors. Ultimately, the article argues that carbon offsetting should be viewed strictly as a last resort for unavoidable emissions, rather than a license for corporations to ignore their primary responsibility to reduce emissions at the source. The practice remains highly relevant to the greenwashing debate because it often serves as a public relations tool that substitutes actual decarbonization with symbolic gestures. As global net-zero deadlines approach, there is an urgent need to reconsider the role of offsets, ensuring they complement rather than replace substantial, direct emission cuts to prevent misleading environmental marketing.
