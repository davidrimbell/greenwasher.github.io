The article defines greenwashing as a deceptive marketing strategy in which companies, particularly in the tourism and tobacco sectors, project an environmental image through superficial actions or misleading labels while continuing harmful practices. This disconnect arises because it is significantly easier for businesses to adjust their communications and packaging aesthetics than to overhaul their production infrastructures, leading to a proliferation of eco-claims that do not reflect genuine sustainability efforts. This practice is relevant to greenwashing because it highlights the gap between perceived and actual environmental impact. By using terms like "eco-friendly" or sponsoring unrelated cleaning initiatives, firms exploit consumer goodwill to mitigate reputational damage without addressing core issues such as excessive water usage, energy consumption, and plastic waste. The article underscores that such tactics often confuse consumers regarding the true meaning of labels, such as "recyclable," thereby prolonging environmental harm rather than resolving it. Ultimately, the text argues that marketing cannot substitute for substantial operational change. While regulatory frameworks exist to penalize these deceptions, the industry’s tendency to prioritize image over infrastructure reveals a systemic issue. True sustainability requires a complete transformation of production models and processes, moving beyond mere communication strategies to address the real ecological footprints of corporate activities.

Does oil-rich Saudi Arabia really want to mend its ways and go green?
The article examines Saudi Arabia’s ambitious Neom project, specifically the futuristic linear city known as The Line, which claims to offer a zero-carbon, car-free urban model. While the initiative presents itself as a global leader in green infrastructure and sustainable urban development, significant skepticism surrounds its feasibility and environmental impact. Critics argue that the linear design disrupts wildlife migration and that the projected benefits may not materialize, raising questions about the genuine sustainability of such grandiose engineering feats. This scenario illustrates greenwashing by highlighting the disparity between lofty environmental branding and practical reality. The project serves as a high-profile vehicle for Saudi Arabia to rebrand its international image, shifting focus away from its oil-dependent economy toward a narrative of progressive modernization and ecological responsibility. By investing heavily in visual spectacles like The Line, the state attempts to signal a commitment to net-zero goals, despite the immense logistical challenges and potential ecological harm posed by the construction itself. The relevance to greenwashing lies in how these massive projects function as symbolic gestures rather than substantive solutions. The article suggests that while there are genuine efforts to liberalize society and increase renewable energy capacity, these initiatives are often intertwined with political maneuvering and public relations campaigns. Consequently, the distinction between sincere environmental adaptation and strategic image management becomes blurred, allowing nations to project an eco-conscious identity while maintaining authoritarian control and continuing fossil fuel reliance.

El riesgo del greenwashing en ASG | El Universal
The growing global emphasis on Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) risks has compelled organizations to integrate sustainability into their core financial reporting and operational strategies. As international standards converge and green financing expands, companies must align their risk management and finance teams to disclose their environmental impact. This shift is transforming internal corporate structures, making sustainability a fundamental aspect of business administration rather than a peripheral concern. However, this heightened focus on environmental claims has inadvertently created a significant new threat: greenwashing, or eco-imposture. This practice involves misleading the public by exaggerating or fabricating environmental initiatives. With the stakes for sustainability performance now higher, companies face intense pressure to appear responsible, leading some to prioritize superficial image over genuine action. This discrepancy between actual environmental stewardship and public perception constitutes a serious ethical breach and a major reputational hazard for enterprises. This phenomenon is particularly relevant to greenwashing because it highlights the danger of decoupling form from substance. The article warns that without rigorous external validation and strong governmental commitment, organizations may exploit vague declarations to conceal a lack of real progress. Ultimately, greenwashing serves as a warning that appearing sustainable can become a shield for inaction, risking catastrophic environmental outcomes while allowing companies to maintain a false sense of integrity in the eyes of investors and the public.
