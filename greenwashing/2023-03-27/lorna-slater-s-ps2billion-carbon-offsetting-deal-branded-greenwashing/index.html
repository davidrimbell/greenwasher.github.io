Lorna Slater's £2billion carbon offsetting deal branded 'greenwashing'

This article highlights a controversial Scottish government initiative partnering with private banks to fund tree planting and bog restoration through carbon offsetting. While the government claims this approach mobilizes essential private investment for environmental restoration, critics argue it creates a lucrative market for tax avoidance. The scheme allows polluting companies to purchase carbon credits from landowners, enabling them to claim net-zero status without actually reducing their emissions. The core implication is that this model facilitates greenwashing by allowing big businesses to buy their way out of responsibility rather than cutting their carbon footprint. Experts and political opponents warn that this validates a system where wealthy investors and offshore entities profit from Scotland’s natural assets. By selling these credits, polluters can continue their harmful activities while presenting an environmentally friendly image, effectively shifting the burden of climate action onto landowners and the public sector. Relevance to greenwashing lies in the mechanism of decoupling financial gain from genuine environmental progress. The article demonstrates how complex financial instruments and public-private partnerships can mask the lack of real emission reductions. It serves as a cautionary example of how well-intentioned conservation efforts can be exploited by corporations to maintain their status quo, undermining true sustainability goals through semantic manipulation and financial engineering rather than operational change.
