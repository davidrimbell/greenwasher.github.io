'Greenshouting’ risks amplifying spin, not transparency, in Asia: experts
The article argues that the Western trend of “greenshouting,” where companies actively highlight their sustainability achievements, is often ineffective or counterproductive in Asia. Instead of fostering transparency, this approach frequently exacerbates greenwashing by allowing corporations to treat Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) metrics as image management tools. Deep-seated cultural norms prioritizing reputation and face-saving lead firms to sanitize disclosures and manage narratives rather than provide honest, verifiable accounts of their environmental impact. This dynamic creates a significant gap between public commitments and actual performance, resulting in what experts term “theatrical” sustainability. Because reporting functions are often situated within public relations teams focused on storytelling rather than risk interrogation, companies produce polished but substantively hollow reports. Consequently, mandatory disclosure regulations are easily met on paper without ensuring robust accountability, enabling businesses to maintain control over their image while avoiding scrutiny of their true operational realities. The relevance to greenwashing lies in the shift toward “greenhushing,” where firms deliberately downplay their efforts to avoid backlash or because consumer demand prioritizes price over ethics. With many brands removing visible sustainability messaging to focus on operational integration, the risk of subtle greenwashing persists. This article highlights that without structural governance changes and verifiable data, increased vocalization or silence alike can obscure rather than clarify corporate environmental responsibilities, making it harder for stakeholders to distinguish genuine progress from strategic omission.

Forever Chemicals Probe Exposes Fashion Greenwashing
The investigation into Lululemon’s potential use of toxic PFAS chemicals exposes a critical disconnect between fashion brands’ wellness marketing and their actual product safety. While the company denies wrongdoing, the case illustrates how companies charge premium prices based on health-conscious imagery, even when their garments may contain harmful substances that accumulate in the body. This highlights the danger of assuming that "clean" labels guarantee non-toxic materials, revealing a significant gap between corporate promises and chemical reality. This issue underscores the mechanics of greenwashing, where vague terms like "sustainable" or "conscious" are used as marketing tools without legal definitions or verified standards. Research indicates that a substantial portion of fashion industry green claims are misleading, relying on self-regulation and voluntary certifications that lack independent oversight. Consequently, consumers are left unable to distinguish genuine environmental responsibility from clever PR strategies, as brands face minimal consequences for failing to substantiate their environmental assertions. The article is relevant to greenwashing because it demonstrates how the lack of mandatory regulations allows the fashion sector to exploit consumer trust in wellness narratives. Without enforceable laws requiring proof for environmental claims, "sustainability" remains a subjective marketing choice rather than a verified guarantee. The situation calls for stricter regulatory frameworks to prevent companies from selling harmful products under the guise of ethical consumption, emphasizing that current voluntary systems are insufficient to protect either public health or genuine sustainability efforts.

Superannuation greenwashing is rife: Will a new labelling system make it worse? - CHOICE
AustralianSuper has reversed its previous divestment from coal, becoming a major shareholder in Whitehaven Coal while maintaining net-zero claims. Critics argue this contradiction exemplifies greenwashing, as the fund’s massive stake in expanding fossil fuel operations undermines its environmental promises. This discrepancy has disillusioned members who feel misled by vague sustainability messaging, prompting some to withdraw their funds in search of genuinely ethical alternatives. The controversy coincides with the Australian government’s consultation on new financial product labeling schemes aimed at standardizing terms like “green” and “sustainable.” Consumer advocates warn that proposed frameworks, which may rely on loose principles or allow limited fossil fuel exposure, could lower the bar for substantiation. They argue this approach risks making it harder to detect and enforce anti-greenwashing laws, effectively benefiting industry marketing teams at the expense of consumer transparency. This article is critically relevant to greenwashing because it highlights the tension between corporate rhetoric and actual investment behavior. It illustrates how ambiguous labeling regimes can enable institutions to market products as environmentally friendly while continuing to support industries contradictory to climate goals. Without rigorous, scientifically based criteria and genuine consumer input, new regulations may institutionalize misleading practices rather than eliminate them.
