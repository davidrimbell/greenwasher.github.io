Superannuation greenwashing is rife: Will a new labelling system make it worse? - CHOICE

AustralianSuper has reversed its previous divestment from coal, becoming a major shareholder in Whitehaven Coal while maintaining net-zero claims. Critics argue this contradiction exemplifies greenwashing, as the fund’s massive stake in expanding fossil fuel operations undermines its environmental promises. This discrepancy has disillusioned members who feel misled by vague sustainability messaging, prompting some to withdraw their funds in search of genuinely ethical alternatives. The controversy coincides with the Australian government’s consultation on new financial product labeling schemes aimed at standardizing terms like “green” and “sustainable.” Consumer advocates warn that proposed frameworks, which may rely on loose principles or allow limited fossil fuel exposure, could lower the bar for substantiation. They argue this approach risks making it harder to detect and enforce anti-greenwashing laws, effectively benefiting industry marketing teams at the expense of consumer transparency. This article is critically relevant to greenwashing because it highlights the tension between corporate rhetoric and actual investment behavior. It illustrates how ambiguous labeling regimes can enable institutions to market products as environmentally friendly while continuing to support industries contradictory to climate goals. Without rigorous, scientifically based criteria and genuine consumer input, new regulations may institutionalize misleading practices rather than eliminate them.
