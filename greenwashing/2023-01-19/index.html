¿Cuán ecológico es ese producto? Conozca cuatro maneras de detectar el "greenwashing" | Diario Financiero
The European Union is leading efforts to combat greenwashing by identifying and regulating vague and misleading environmental claims. With more than half of the analyzed eco-claims found to be unfounded, the proposed regulation seeks to require verifiable scientific evidence, implying a structural shift in how companies communicate their environmental impact in order to restore consumer trust. The proposed regulation specifically targets the use of terms such as “carbon neutral” or “more eco-friendly,” which often mask reliance on questionable offsets or comparisons lacking a clear basis. By prohibiting unspecific superlatives, the EU mandates that companies disclose both the genuine environmental benefits and harms, preventing them from using marginal or long-standing improvements to disguise current harmful practices. Furthermore, the regulation demands transparency regarding interim targets and material composition, eliminating ambiguity in concepts like “biodegradable” or “compostable.” This measure is crucial because such labels frequently mislead consumers about the true sustainability of products, particularly concerning plastics and bioplastics. By requiring specificity about the origin and conditions of decomposition, the regulation dismantles marketing traps that distract from the real impact of the supply chain. This article is relevant because it demonstrates how greenwashing persists through the strategic use of ambiguous language that confuses consumers. The EU’s legislative response sets a global precedent in which clarity and scientific verifiability replace vague marketing, forcing corporations to align their communications with their actual actions rather than merely favorable perceptions.

Greenwashing or a net zero necessity? Climate scientists on carbon offsetting
Prominent climate scientists present a nuanced critique of carbon offsetting, revealing how the mechanism often serves as a loophole for corporations to avoid genuine decarbonization. While some experts acknowledge that offsetting can fund vital nature-based solutions like forest protection, they emphasize that it must never substitute for immediate, drastic emission cuts. This distinction is crucial because when companies treat offsets as a license to maintain high pollution levels, they undermine the urgent scientific pathways required to prevent catastrophic warming. The integrity of carbon markets is heavily questioned due to widespread fraud and a lack of regulation, allowing companies to purchase "hot air" credits that fail to deliver real environmental benefits. Experts warn that without strict oversight, these markets enable greenwashing by creating an illusion of sustainability while the underlying drivers of destruction, such as industrial agriculture and fossil fuel dependence, remain unchecked. Consequently, offsetting risks becoming a tool for maintaining the status quo rather than facilitating the necessary transition to a low-carbon economy. Critically, offsetting is identified as actively dangerous because it encourages a rebound effect, where individuals and corporations feel justified in expanding carbon-intensive activities under the false assurance of neutrality. Since trees take decades to sequester carbon, they cannot mitigate the immediate climate impact of present-day emissions, leading to a dangerous time lag that exacerbates global heating. This perspective highlights that offsetting often masks permanent carbon transfers with temporary fixes, rendering it an inadequate and potentially harmful strategy for achieving true climate justice and net-zero goals.

Insurance industry net-zero protocol launched at Davos 'opens door to greenwashing': NGO
The Net-Zero Insurance Alliance has introduced a new protocol aiming to align insurers with the Paris Agreement’s 1.5°C warming limit. Proponents hail this framework as a landmark step toward ambitious climate targets, expecting members to disclose initial decarbonization goals. By establishing standardized accounting methods for insurance portfolios, the initiative seeks to guide the industry’s asset divestment from fossil fuels, theoretically supporting the energy transition as extreme weather events increasingly threaten consumer coverage stability. However, environmental activists argue the protocol facilitates corporate greenwashing by failing to mandate reductions in Scope 3 emissions. These upstream emissions, generated by customers burning fossil fuels that insurers cover, represent a significantly larger carbon footprint than operational emissions. Critics note that the framework allows for low ambition, permitting targets far below scientific requirements, and excludes coverage for new power plants. This enables insurers to claim climate leadership while continuing to finance the very projects driving global warming. This controversy highlights the critical tension between industry self-regulation and genuine climate action within the financial sector. The lack of comprehensive emission accounting allows businesses to appear sustainable without addressing their most significant environmental impact. As insurance losses from climate disasters rise, the relevance of this debate intensifies, underscoring the need for rigorous standards that prevent mere rhetoric from masking continued fossil fuel support in the race to limit global temperature increases.
