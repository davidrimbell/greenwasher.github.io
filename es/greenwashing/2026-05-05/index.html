Del “Greenwashing” al silencio: el auge del “Greenhushing” como refugio corporativo
The article highlights a significant shift from greenwashing to "greenhushing," where companies increasingly silence their sustainability efforts to avoid regulatory scrutiny and accusations of misleading practices. This trend transforms environmental commitment from a competitive advantage into a perceived risk, prompting many firms, particularly in fashion, energy, and banking, to reduce their public communication despite achieving real progress. While this caution stems from stricter European regulations requiring rigorous proof of environmental claims, the total silence creates substantial market vulnerabilities. By withdrawing from the conversation, brands risk losing positive consumer perception and ceding ground to competitors who communicate effectively. Furthermore, this retreat can inadvertently breed distrust, as consumers may interpret the lack of transparency as a confession of guilt or a lack of genuine effort. To avoid these pitfalls, the piece argues that the solution is not less communication, but more rigorous and coherent communication. Brands must integrate sustainability and marketing teams to ensure messages are backed by concrete data and align with actual business practices. Ultimately, maintaining transparency about both achievements and ongoing challenges is essential to building credibility and protecting against greenwashing allegations in an increasingly critical market.

Qué es el greenwashing y cómo las empresas dicen ser ecológicas para atraer consumidores
The article identifies greenwashing as a deceptive strategy where companies project a false image of environmental commitment to capitalize on consumer demand for sustainability, without implementing actual changes in their production processes. This phenomenon is increasingly prevalent, particularly in markets with limited regulation, as firms prioritize changing their discourse over transforming their business models to attract ethically minded buyers. To detect this manipulation, experts highlight warning signs such as the use of vague terminology, imagery of nature lacking data support, and the emphasis on minor sustainable actions that overshadow larger negative impacts. True sustainability requires verification through external certifications and transparent, audited reports rather than appealing packaging or superficial claims, emphasizing that transparency and measurable data are the only reliable indicators of genuine environmental responsibility. The relevance of this discussion to greenwashing lies in its broader societal impact: it fosters deep consumer distrust and creates unfair competition against genuinely sustainable companies. By normalizing deception, this practice not only misleads the public but also disincentivizes real ecological change. Ultimately, both companies and consumers share responsibility, as the latter’s reliance on visual cues over verification allows the cycle of mistrust and environmental harm to persist.
